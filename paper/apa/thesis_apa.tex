\textbf{Abstract}

% APA 7th: Abstract is a single unindented paragraph
{
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
Pulmonary Tuberculosis (PTB) still lingers as a big public health concern in the Cordilleras, where differences between patients and gaps in the treatment program still contribute to treatment failure. This study develops and evaluates machine learning models to predict treatment success or failure among patients in DOH-CAR TB-DOTS facilities for the years from 2015 to 2025. A preprocessing pipeline performs the following: schema harmonization, patient pseudonymization (for anonymization), and a diagnostic-first missing data handling protocol. The protocol classifies each variable's missingness mechanism as Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), Missing at Random (MAR), or Missing Not at Random (MNAR), then routes the variable to one of four strategies: column exclusion, listwise deletion, missing indicator imputation, or stochastic Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). Supervised tree-based classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, and Random Forest) are trained alongside Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) temporal architectures to model longitudinal treatment dynamics over the 6- to 12-month treatment course. Because clinical decision support needs more than an accurate risk score, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) are used to show which patient-level factors contributed to each prediction so that clinicians can review the system's outputs. The validated models are embedded in a Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) that provides dynamic risk estimates, SHAP-grounded factor explanations, and bounded narrative summaries. The CDSS is intended to support early clinical intervention while preserving clinician judgment.

Pulmonary Tuberculosis (PTB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by \textit{Mycobacterium tuberculosis} (MTB), which is a slow-growing, acid-fast bacillus. And because the disease progresses gradually and that treatment can fail, mitigation is difficult . Effective treatments exist, but PTB is still widespread. It tracks closely with poverty, which is naturally followed by undernutrition, and comorbidities as well such as diabetes and HIV, all of which weaken patients' immune defenses and raise susceptibility to the disease . These conditions also delay the diagnosis and complicate treatment. TB is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, and in 2018 alone, there were around 10 million new cases and 1.5 million deaths, and about half a million of those were resistant to rifampicin.

In the Philippines, TB still remains as a big problem with regards to public health, and the World Health Organization (WHO) lists the country as one of the biggest contributors to the global TB burden, estimated at around 6.8 percent of cases coming from the Philippines. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, Philippine TB rates were also among the highest in Asia: over 554 cases per 100,000 people in 2019 . In 2023, about 37,000 Filipinos died of TB out of 739,000 diagnosed cases , and the WHO classifies the country as a high-burden setting for both MDR-TB and TB/HIV co-infection . That same year the DOH logged 575,770 TB cases in its Integrated TB Information System. The number itself signals heavy under-registration and underdiagnosis . For treatment, the DOH follows CDC-recommended regimens for active and latent TB under the TB-DOTS strategy, widened in 2003 to take in private clinics, pharmacies, and hospitals . Gaps persist even so, in diagnosis, in treatment monitoring, in patient support, and most of all in managing MDR-TB .

Baguio City shows the same gap up close. In 2022 the DOH-CAR confirmed 437 TB cases there but counted more than 4,405 that were missing or never reported, which puts the city near the top for surveillance and treatment work . Passive case-finding, stigma, and the cost of care drive that gap, and the undetected patients keep spreading the disease in their communities .

The large population and a lot of daily traffic (both foot and vehicular) in the city may increase opportunities for tuberculosis to spread. The colder climate can also create more ideal conditions for the transmission of \textit{Mycobacterium tuberculosis}. The city also has a more inflated local load of cases because it takes in more complex tuberculosis cases from neighboring provinces because of it being a tertiary referral center . Patient-level factors also play an important role in treatment outcomes such as comorbidities like diabetes mellitus, HIV infection, and malnutrition, all of which weaken their immune response and may reduce treatment completion. Food insecurity, poor housing conditions, and transportation costs can also interrupt adherence to directly observed treatment short-course (DOTS) .

All in all, these constraints argue for strategies that work on more than one facet of assessment. Treatment failure not only worsens the outcome for the singular patient, it also extends the period for which transmission can happen in the community and in-turn speeds up the emergence of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), defined as resistance to at least rifampicin and isoniazid. MDR-TB requires regimens that are longer, more toxic, and more expensive, and it carries much lower success rates. The result is a wider threat to regional disease control in the Philippines .
\subsection{Risk Factors for Treatment Failure}

To ensure consistent evaluation of tuberculosis control efforts, the Department of Health (DOH) established standardized criteria for classifying treatment outcomes through the National Tuberculosis Program (NTP). These outcome definitions are important for monitoring program performance, comparing findings across studies, and identifying factors associated with treatment outcomes. Treatment success and failure do not occur randomly, as they are often influenced by identifiable patient, clinical, and program-related risk factors. Mortality remains the primary outcome examined in most studies on treatment failure, although some also include disease progression, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, and loss to follow-up as indicators of poor treatment outcomes .

One of the risk factors is close or direct contact with patients who have active TB infection where patients who acquired the infection through close contact were at higher risk of delayed diagnosis and more likely to be non-adherent, either by refusing or quitting the regimen . Another risk factor is recurrence of the infection or a history of receiving TB treatment because patients who missed follow-ups had a higher risk of reactivation and mortality, and were harder to treat than patients with no prior history of TB . Frequent missed or interrupted treatment also leads to drug resistance .

Comorbidities have consistently shown a significant influence on tuberculosis treatment outcomes across different settings. In Eastern Ethiopia, HIV co-infection was a substantial risk factor for treatment failure because the weakened immune system makes treatment less effective . TB and HIV drug interactions also accelerate each other's progression and increase the occurrence of side effects, which can compromise treatment success . A study in Davao City, found a higher risk of treatment failure in comorbid patients: 58.82\% of the 51 patients who experienced treatment failure had at least one comorbidity . Another major factor is Diabetes Mellitus (DM). TB patients with diagnosed DM have a higher risk of treatment failure, relapse, and death than non-diabetics . In a systematic review and meta-analysis, DM patients had a higher risk of treatment failure for Drug-resistant (DR) TB and mortality for MDR-TB, with odds ratios of 1.56 and 1.57 respectively . Another study reported that patients with both PTB and chronic conditions, especially DM, had reduced rates of sputum conversion and a higher probability of poor treatment outcomes, including death and progression to MDR-TB .

Taken together, these results say that no single factor drives TB treatment failure. It grows out of the way comorbidities, programmatic gaps, and socioeconomic strain feed into each other, and a one-factor analysis cannot see that. Catching it calls for a machine learning framework able to model the interactions all at once, the kind of model that makes earlier, better-aimed clinical intervention possible .

\subsection{Machine Learning for TB Outcome Prediction}

Previous studies on tuberculosis treatment outcomes have focused on sociodemographic characteristics, comorbidities, medical history, and other patient-level risk factors. The findings remain inconsistent and fragmented because methodological constraints limit how these factors are analyzed together. Most studies rely on traditional statistical analyses such as statistical tests or regression models, which examine one factor at a time, or a limited set of factors, while assuming linear relationships among variables . These approaches provide important evidence on individual risk factors, but they may miss the combined and non-linear effects that shape treatment success or failure.

This is where machine learning (ML) pulls ahead of conventional statistics: an algorithm that learns from high-dimensional data can pick up the subtle dependencies that decide whether treatment holds or fails. The variables in play, demographic, clinical, diagnostic, treatment-related, interact in complex, non-linear ways, the exact patterns a single-factor method tends to miss. Plenty of algorithms have been tried on the problem, logistic regression, Support Vector Machines (SVM), ensemble methods like Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) . Within TB, earlier work has aimed ML at predicting drug resistance, flagging high-risk patients, and tuning treatment strategy . The lesson across all of it is plain: a predictive model can carry risk-factor analysis further by reading several dimensions of patient data at once.

A clinical decision support system has to be interpretable. An ML model that hands back only an end result, with no account of why, is of little use at the bedside: a healthcare worker needs to understand the basis of a risk alert before acting on it. Tell TB-DOTS staff a patient is high-risk without naming the factors behind it, and they have little to trust or act on. The study answers this with Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), a post-hoc technique built on Shapley values that quantifies how much each variable contributed to a prediction and rolls up into both patient-level explanations and overall feature importance . It suits tree-based clinical models like XGBoost, Random Forest, and LightGBM, where accuracy can be high but the internal decision path is hard to inspect. In the healthcare ML literature, SHAP is among the most used explanation methods, tabular data especially . Work on clinical ML explainability has leaned on it to surface real contributors to treatment failure: drug resistance, TB history, lab markers, and more .

Fragmented risk-factor studies hit a ceiling, and interpretable ML is the way past it. Reading what drives TB outcomes means combining several dimensions of patient data, not inspecting them one by one. The study builds a multi-layer framework over demographic, clinical, diagnostic, and treatment-related variables. Descriptive analysis lays out patient profiles and treatment patterns; supervised models estimate outcome risk; SHAP explanations show which factors moved each estimate. Accuracy goes up, and the output stays legible to providers , who can then see who is at higher risk of failure, why the model says so, and where to step in early with closer monitoring and better management.

\subsection{Contributions}

The DOH-CAR TB-DOTS program runs on the National Tuberculosis Program (NTP) protocol. Case finding is passive and begins at the facility: someone with TB symptoms, a cough that will not quit, weight loss, fever, is sent for testing, sputum smear microscopy, the GeneXpert MTB/RIF test, and a chest X-ray . After diagnosis the patient goes onto the Treatment Card and Master List, gets a registration group, and is paired with a health worker or trained treatment partner who watches each dose over the six- to twelve-month course . Every month, a visit records sputum results, vital signs, and weight, and checks that the patient is keeping up. The outcome is later filed under NTP criteria, Cured, Treatment Completed, Failed, Died, or Lost to Follow-up . Most of this is paper, and risk is read in hindsight. A worsening condition, missed doses, a complication from another illness, these tend to come to light only after they have already bent the course of treatment. By then the high-risk patient was never flagged in time, and the cost is avoidable failures, more deaths, and continued spread of drug-resistant (MDR) TB across the region .

The research presents six major contributions. The first contribution is developing and validating an Integrated Predictive Model utilizing machine learning models that combine training sets based upon demographic, clinical, diagnostic and treatment related variables to forecast therapy success or failure of PTB patients in CAR. Therefore giving clinicians the opportunity to identify potential problem patients earlier.

Secondly, a reproducible Data Preprocessing \& Harmonization Pipeline which takes raw patient information from TB-DOTS clinics throughout the CAR and transforms the datasets for machine learning algorithms' use, using a variety of techniques such as schema normalization; pseudonymous identification of individual patients; automatic correction of errors; and a diagnostic-first approach to handle missing values.

Thirdly, a Descriptive/Exploratory Analysis that describes the study population; provides insight into how the various attributes relate to one another; and identifies the most important predictors of treatment outcomes.

Fourthly, Temporal Modeling to examine time dependent relationships in the monthly monitoring data using Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) architectures and therefore capture variations that occur over time during treatment. Therefore, temporal modeling is expected to generate predictive accuracy that improves upon prior studies which did not account for temporal changes in monitoring data.

Fifthly, Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) were used to measure the degree to which each patient-specific factor contributed to a prediction. SHAP are also useful because they allow clinicians to see what specific factors led to each predicted risk's score and thus help evaluate whether or not a particular recommendation should be implemented for each patient.

Lastly, the validated model was embedded into a Clinical Decision Support system (CDSS) that displays a patient's current level of risk; lists all the factors that contribute to each risk assessment via SHAP; and updates a patient's risk estimate when new follow-up data becomes available.

\subsection{Research Objectives}

This study aims to develop and validate machine learning models that predict treatment success or failure among TB patients in the CAR, Philippines. Combining demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables supports early identification of patients at risk of unsuccessful treatment and provides evidence-based tools for improving TB program outcomes. The broader goal is to enable personalized clinical interventions and efficient allocation of healthcare resources. The research aims to achieve the following objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To document and pre-process patient records of PTB cases from TB-DOTS facilities in the CAR through data cleaning, feature engineering, and reporting, so that the dataset is ready for ML applications.
    \item To describe the demographic, clinical, diagnostic, and treatment characteristics of PTB patients in the dataset and provide an overview of the study population.
    \item To identify variables that influence treatment outcomes over the course of therapy, so that the most important factors affecting patient recovery can be recognized and used to guide treatment adjustments.
    \item To add temporal modeling that analyzes time-dependent patterns in patient data, so that changes and trends throughout treatment can be detected, improving the accuracy of outcome predictions and supporting timely clinical interventions.
    \item To train and validate supervised learning models for predicting treatment success or failure among PTB patients, so that individuals at risk of poor outcomes can be identified early, while the model maintains high predictive accuracy and reliability when applied to incoming TB cases.
    \item To develop a decision support tool that visualizes patient risk levels and predictive outputs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

The dataset includes PTB patients from 2015 to 2025 in the CAR, specifically Baguio City, and excludes those still undergoing treatment at the time of data extraction. Only patients with a defined treatment outcome of either success or failure are included. The data covers demographic information, laboratory tests, diagnostic data, clinical data, treatment regimen, follow-up examinations, drug administration data, and treatment outcomes. The dataset's limits are mostly about sufficiency and quality. Because it comes from existing records, completeness and consistency vary, and some clinical parameters are simply incomplete, which narrows the set of risk factors available and weakens parts of the analysis. It also will not fully mirror the current patient population, since anyone still in treatment was left out. Extraction from DOH-CAR brings its own friction. Across the ten years from 2015 to 2025, record-keeping shifts as the health system and its documentation standards change, so consistency is not guaranteed. And where indicators are self-reported, bias and inconsistency can seep in and dull the model's predictions.

\subsection{Significance of the Study}

The models are meant to help providers spot the patients most likely to fail treatment, early enough for a closer look, tighter monitoring, and better management. Whether clinicians adopt the tool turns on whether they can read and judge its output, so the framework leans on explainable prediction rather than a bare risk score. SHAP surfaces the patient-level factors behind each estimate, and the CDSS lays them out in a form a clinician can actually work from. The larger aim is to strengthen TB control in the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) by turning data the program already collects into tools that predict, explain, and can be acted on. Concretely, the results should sharpen patient management and treatment planning, firm up how the program runs, and give public health decisions better footing for lifting the TB treatment success rate in CAR. The study also means to put something usable in administrators' hands, a model that could grow into a working application for the local TB-DOTS program. Alongside it, a cleaned and pseudonymized derivative of the DOH-CAR PTB Treatment Outcome Dataset will be released for clinical, epidemiological, and machine learning research.

\section{Method}

This study draws on PTB patient records from the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR), Philippines, covering 2015 to 2025 and held centrally by the Department of Health-CAR (DOH-CAR). Work began once ethical approval came through. Records were pseudonymized first, then run through a standardized preprocessing pipeline (schema harmonization, error correction, feature scaling) and the diagnostic-first missing data protocol set out later in the Missing Data Handling subsection. Exploratory and cluster analyses describe the cohort and produce subgroup-level features. Ensemble tree-based classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest) then learn to predict treatment outcomes as defined by National Tuberculosis Program criteria. Treatment plays out over months rather than a single visit, so temporal models, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), are tested beside the static ones and ensembled for robustness. Standard classification metrics measure performance. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) carry interpretability. The final models feed a clinical decision support framework that revises each risk estimate as treatment goes on. Figure~\ref{fig:data_management} gives the overview.

The other is a temporal monitoring dataset, compiled by hand by the study's Pharmacy Co-authors, that trains the longitudinal deep learning and temporal tree-based models. \subsubsection*{Non-Temporal Dataset}

DOH-CAR builds this dataset centrally, pulling reports from government TB-DOTS facilities and from private practitioners in the Public-Private Mix DOTS (PPMD) scheme . There are 7,389 patient records, baseline features only: demographics (age, sex, civil status), clinical indicators (BMI, blood pressure), diagnostic results (GeneXpert, Smear Microscopy). Each row is one patient at the point of treatment registration, with no follow-up monitoring attached. This is the main training source for the static classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest).

The non-temporal pipeline harmonizes variable formats and units, then pseudonymizes the records. The binary target comes straight from standardized National Tuberculosis Program (NTP) criteria: `Cured' or `Treatment Completed' map to `Success'; `Failed', `Died', or `Lost to Follow-up' map to `Failure'. Continuous clinical variables get their extreme outliers capped so training is not skewed. Whatever is missing passes through the diagnostic-first protocol described later in the Missing Data Handling subsection.

Splitting into training and testing sets happens before any transformation, which keeps test information out of the model. The split is stratified, so success and failure proportions stay level across both partitions. Everything downstream, feature scaling, normalization, the multivariate imputation of missing values, is fit on the training set and nothing else. Those fitted parameters and imputation rules then transfer to the held-out test set. No model ever sees the test data's distribution.

\subsubsection*{Temporal Monitoring Dataset}

The temporal monitoring dataset is a longitudinal subset of 599 patients, each with complete month-by-month records. The DOH-CAR registry held nothing like it. Instead, the study's Pharmacy Co-authors \textit{gathered and encoded it by hand} from paper Treatment Cards and Master Lists at TB-DOTS centers in Baguio City, Benguet, going to the facilities in person and transcribing each patient's monitoring data: demographics, monthly vital signs, percentage adherence, doses taken and missed, smear microscopy (TB-LAMP) and Xpert MTB/RIF results, and treatment outcomes. Everything landed in a master \texttt{RAW DATA.xlsx} workbook, one sheet per barangay or facility unit, across 35 facility-year cycles from 2015 to 2025. A consolidation script, \texttt{combine\_raw\_dataset.py}, then merged the per-barangay sheets, normalizing facility names against a canonical alias map so mismatched entries lined up before the main pipeline ran.

The layout is wide: one row per patient, columns holding monthly observations from treatment Month 0 (M0) through Month 12 (M12). Per monthly visit, the columns record percentage adherence (\texttt{pct\_adherence}), doses taken and missed that month, cumulative doses taken, body weight, height, smear microscopy (TB-LAMP), and the Xpert MTB/RIF result. Static baseline features sit alongside the monthly ones on the same row: demographics, baseline vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, temperature, respiratory rate), comorbidities, the starting treatment regimen, and diagnostic classification. After preprocessing, the cleaned table is 599 rows by 269 columns.

The temporal pipeline has eight stages, shaped around this wide longitudinal layout:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Schema Validation \& Column Standardization.} Column names are forced to \texttt{snake\_case}. Duplicates and fully null columns get flagged and dropped.
  \item \textbf{Identifier Removal.} Four patient- and facility-identifying columns (\texttt{no}, \texttt{source\_file}, \texttt{name\_of\_diagnosing\_facility}, \texttt{name\_of\_treatment\_unit}) stay in the export file for traceability but never reach any model input, which is how patient privacy is enforced.
  \item \textbf{Data Cleaning \& Type Coercion.} Every column is parsed to its correct type and clinically validated. Domain-specific formats get their own parsers: weight strings (\texttt{45kg}, \texttt{56.5 kg}) become numeric kilograms; heights become centimeters; blood pressure in \texttt{120/80} form splits into separate systolic and diastolic fields; oxygen saturation arrives as a decimal fraction (0.95), a percent-suffixed value (\texttt{95\%}), or a raw integer, and all three collapse to one 0 to 100 scale; adherence percentages lose any trailing \texttt{\%}. Dates get a correction pass of their own. A future date of birth is pulled back (e.g., 2063 \textrightarrow{} 1963), transposed digits are caught (e.g., 2990 \textrightarrow{} 1990), and ordering is enforced (treatment start $\geq$ diagnosis date). Continuous clinical variables are then winsorized to physiologically plausible bounds: temperature to 30 to 45$^\circ$C, systolic BP to 60 to 220 mmHg, diastolic BP to 30 to 140 mmHg, heart rate to 20 to 250 bpm, oxygen saturation to 70 to 100\%, weight to 10 to 180 kg, height to 80 to 220 cm, respiratory rate to 4 to 60 breaths/min.
  \item \textbf{Categorical Harmonization.} Inconsistent categorical entries are mapped onto canonical labels. Outcome variants, sex designations, regimen types, comorbidity flags, civil status: each collapses from hundreds of free-text variants down to its small expected set.
  \item \textbf{Drop Uninformative Columns.} Columns that are near-entirely null or redundant (e.g., \texttt{nationality\_raw} once it has merged into \texttt{nationality}) are flagged in the output, not silently deleted, so the cleaning decisions stay auditable.
  \item \textbf{Wide \textrightarrow{} Long Temporal Restructuring.} The wide table is melted into two: a static baseline table (\texttt{df\_static}, one row per patient) and a temporal table (\texttt{df\_temporal}, one row per patient-month) with columns \texttt{[patient\_id, month, weight, height, pct\_adherence, monthly\_doses\_taken, monthly\_missed\_doses, cumulative\_doses\_taken, smear\_tb\_lamp, xpert\_mtb\_rif]}.
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic-First Missing Data Handling.} The four-pathway diagnostic pipeline from the Missing Data Handling subsection runs independently on the static and temporal feature sets. Temporal features are never hard-excluded for being sparse. Their missingness is structural, later months simply carry fewer observations as patients finish or abandon treatment, and that pattern is preserved with missingness indicators.
  \item \textbf{Post-Imputation Clinical Clipping \& Export.} Clinical bounds are re-applied to every imputed value (e.g., weight to 10 to 180 kg, pct\_adherence to 0 to 100). A monotonicity repair pass undoes any MICE-introduced dip in \texttt{cumulative\_doses\_taken} within a patient. The pipeline then writes \texttt{cleaned\_human\_readable.csv} (599 rows $\times$ 269 columns), original clinical units and category labels intact. Encoding, scaling, and tensor preparation wait for the model-specific scripts that run after the train-test split, deliberately, so nothing leaks.
\end{enumerate}

Same as the non-temporal case, the temporal train-test split comes before any scaling, one-hot encoding, outlier capping, or 3-dimensional tensor construction for the recurrent networks. Both pipelines guard against leakage in the same way, static classifier or temporal network alike. \subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis}

An exploratory pass came before any modeling. It profiled the study population, gauged how complete the data was, and measured how the variables associated with each other. Those findings shaped the preprocessing and modeling choices that followed.

\input{tables/missing_data_summary.tex}

A global missingness audit ran over all 28 columns and ranked each by its missing rate (Table~\ref{tab:missing_data_summary}). Some variables were badly incomplete. Microscopy result was missing 37.8\% of the time, the diagnosis-derived temporal features 53.6\%, and the clinical delay intervals anywhere from 59.3\% up to 98.8\%. Those numbers fixed the missingness thresholds applied later in the Missing Data Handling subsection.

For the continuous variables, pairwise Pearson's r was taken on available-case observations: age, four derived clinical delay intervals (days from screening to diagnosis, to treatment, to microscopy result, and to RDT result), and diagnosis day-of-year. The question behind it was simple, whether MICE was worth the trouble. When continuous predictors actually share correlation structure, MICE can borrow signal from the observed features to estimate the missing ones. When they don't, a plain univariate mean-fill does the same job.

Categorical variables used Cram\'{e}r's V instead, a chi-square-based measure scaled to the \([0, 1]\) interval. Any column with fewer than two unique non-null values, or fewer than ten non-null observations, dropped out of the matrix. The set here was patient demographics (sex, type of TB, anatomical site) and clinical classifications (registration group, bacteriologic status, microscopy results).

The exploratory phase also clusters patients across demographic, clinical, and diagnostic dimensions to surface latent subgroups. K-prototypes does the work, since it handles the dataset's mix of categorical and continuous variables. The number of clusters is settled iteratively from the Silhouette score and the elbow method together. That lets the analysis separate groups by comorbidity profile, adherence behavior, and how they respond to a regimen.

The cluster assignments do double duty: descriptive interpretation, and engineered features for the supervised models. The labels are one-hot encoded as extra categorical inputs for the tree-based classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest) and folded into the feature space for the temporal networks (RNN, LSTM). With them, a model can latch onto subgroup-level patterns behind treatment success or failure, not just individual predictors.

The temporal monitoring dataset got the same scrutiny before preprocessing, a global missingness audit over every feature, columns ranked by missing rate. Above 80\% missingness, a column was excluded outright. Between 50\% and 80\%, it went to the missing-indicator-with-fill pathway, weighed against its predictive importance. Threshold triage like this is standard. It fits the handling strategy to how bad the missingness is, rather than forcing one rule across the board .

Patient retention was traced as the share of patients with non-missing adherence data at each interval from M0 through M12. A raw monthly count tells you little, because retention falls naturally over a six- to twelve-month course, so the decay was expressed as a percentage of the starting cohort at each successive month. This also sets the temporal modeling scope. Once fewer than 25\% of patients still have data, that month and every month after it drops out of the feature-level correlation analyses, which keeps a small, non-representative surviving group from driving the estimates.

The same Pearson's r check, again on available-case observations, ran over the vital signs in the temporal dataset. A multivariate imputation only earns its keep when the vitals correlate strongly. If they move together, Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) can borrow signal from the observed features to fill gaps; if they don't, MICE buys nothing over mean- or median-fill . The vitals here were age, heart rate, oxygen saturation, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, weight, height, respiratory rate, and temperature, wherever the records carried them.

To see how the temporal features relate, Pearson correlations were taken on per-patient observed means across Months 0 through 7. Each feature, a dose metric (monthly doses taken, cumulative doses taken, percentage adherence), an anthropometric one (weight, height), or a diagnostic result (smear microscopy, Xpert MTB/RIF), was averaged across a patient's available visits. Retention characteristics came from the same window: the last month attended and the total months observed. Averaging this way is deliberate, because it preserves the dropout signal. A patient who quits after M4 contributes means from M0 to M4 and no further, and that short window carries information of its own rather than a gap waiting to be imputed.

Categorical variables in the temporal dataset again went through Cram\'{e}r's V, the chi-square-based measure on the [0, 1] interval. Pearson and Cram\'{e}r's V were never mixed into a single matrix. Pearson's r assumes interval-level measurement and gives misleading values on nominal or ordinal categories whose spacing is uneven , so keeping Cram\'{e}r's V separate for the categorical features avoids that and yields interpretable estimates. Columns with fewer than two unique non-null values, or fewer than ten non-null observations, were left out; association can't be estimated meaningfully there anyway.

A cardinality audit over every categorical feature in the raw dataset showed how much inconsistent coding had to be fixed. Counting the unique raw values per feature put a number on the entry inconsistency. Where a feature's cardinality ran far past its expected count of valid categories, say a clinical observation field that should carry a handful of standardized codes but instead carried hundreds of free-text variants, it was flagged for harmonization in Stage 4. The audit hands those harmonization targets an empirical baseline: it measures the data entry problem before any cleaning touches it.

Whether adherence holds steady over the course was tested with lag-1 Pearson correlations of percentage adherence across consecutive month pairs (M0 to M1 through M7 to M8). That month-to-month strength decides whether a recurrent architecture, which assumes each time step leans on the one before, is even worth building. Weak correlation between adjacent months would mean a static model on aggregated patient means tells you just as much. Only strong correlation justifies the extra complexity of an RNN or LSTM .

\subsection{Missing Data Handling}

Missingness in clinical TB registries is uneven. It traces to inconsistent documentation, to facilities of differing capacity, and to the simple fact that treatment is monitored over many months. Once the mechanism behind the gaps shifts from feature to feature, imposing a single imputation strategy on all of them is statistically wrong. So this study runs a diagnostic-first pipeline: it classifies how each variable went missing, then sends that variable down one of four routes, column exclusion, listwise deletion, a missing indicator with fill, or stochastic Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) (Figure~\ref{fig:missing_data_pipeline}) .

The first diagnostic step is Little's MCAR test at the dataset level, asking whether the data are Missing Completely at Random (MCAR). Were that true, missingness would be independent of every value, observed or not, the complete cases would be an unbiased subsample, and listwise deletion would be safe. It is not true here. The test came back significant ($p < 0.05$), which rejects MCAR and ties the missingness systematically to the observed data. Deleting cases wholesale is off the table .

Little's test returns one omnibus verdict for the whole dataset, so each column's mechanism still needs its own classification. The pipeline checks every variable's missingness indicator against the observed data. For temporal (monthly) variables, Spearman rank correlations relate the column's monthly missing rate to treatment month; a correlation that climbs monotonically signals a dropout pattern, which is Missing Not at Random (MNAR). For static baseline variables, point-biserial correlations relate the binary is-missing indicator to the observed features. A significant association means other observed variables explain the gap, making it Missing at Random (MAR); a non-significant one points back to MNAR. Classifying column by column, rather than swallowing one dataset-level verdict, lets each handling strategy match the statistics of its own variable .

Two decisions, column exclusion and MICE eligibility, lean on a Random Forest classifier trained on available cases, the rows where the treatment outcome is observed. For ranking alone, features are filled with column medians. It is a coarse fill, but good enough to estimate importance. Variables in the bottom quartile of importance become candidates for soft exclusion; those above the median get priority for stochastic imputation .

A hard threshold drops any variable over 80\% missing on sight, importance aside, because nothing reliable can be imputed at that sparsity. A soft threshold also drops variables past 50\% missing whose importance falls in the bottom quartile, since imputing a sparse low-value variable adds more synthetic variance than information. Temporal variables sidestep both thresholds. Their structural missingness is the longitudinal design of TB monitoring showing through, not a data quality fault .

Listwise deletion stays on the table for one narrow case: a variable confirmed MCAR at the column level, under 15\% missing, with more than 500 patients still left after deletion. In that situation the complete cases are an unbiased subsample, so deletion gives unbiased estimates without the variance distortion that imputation introduces. No variable here cleared all three conditions at once, so the pathway never fired .

Variables classified MNAR, along with every temporal feature regardless of mechanism, get a two-part treatment. First, a binary indicator (\texttt{is\_missing\_[variable]}) marks the absence itself. Then the original variable is filled, last observed value carried forward for temporal variables, column median for continuous static ones, column mode for categorical static ones. The absence is treated as signal, not noise to be papered over. A missing Month 9 weight in TB monitoring usually means the patient dropped out, and the indicator passes exactly that clinical pattern to the model. Backward-fill is barred from temporal imputation: letting a future measurement seep into an earlier time step would leak information the model has no legitimate access to at inference .

Four vital signs cleared both bars, confirmed MAR and ranked above median importance: oxygen saturation, heart rate, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Each is imputed with stochastic MICE under an ExtraTreesRegressor as the conditional model. ExtraTreesRegressor is a nonparametric tree-based estimator. It captures non-linear relationships in mixed-type clinical data without standardized variables, much like the missForest framework , and on this kind of data it usually outperforms linear and $k$-nearest-neighbor imputers. The MICE iteration count tracks each variable's missing fraction, scaling as $\max(20, \min(100, \lfloor \text{miss\_rate} \times 100 \rfloor))$, which gives high-sparsity features room to converge without letting compute run away. That mirrors Von Hippel's rule that the number of imputations should roughly match the percentage of missing information , applied here to iterations instead of to the count of imputed datasets. And imputing stochastically rather than deterministically keeps the natural spread of the imputed variables, instead of collapsing them onto conditional means .

\subsection{Data Quality Assurance}

A layered quality assurance protocol keeps the supervised pipeline reliable. It runs across schema validation, clinical bounds checking, temporal consistency, and post-processing validation.

\subsubsection*{Schema and Column Validation}

Column names are normalized to \texttt{snake\_case}: whitespace stripped, text lowercased, special characters swapped for word equivalents, so \texttt{M0 \%Adherence} turns into \texttt{m0\_pct\_adherence}. Duplicate columns and fully null ones are flagged and deduplicated before modeling. Four patient- and facility-identifying columns remain in the export file for traceability, but none of them reach a model input, for privacy.

\subsubsection*{Date Integrity Correction}

A dedicated correction pass deals with the recording errors that clinical registries tend to accumulate. A future date of birth, its year past the data year, is pulled back 100 years. A date of birth before 1920 is pushed forward 100 years when that puts the age in a plausible 0 to 110 range. Transposed digits, 2990 for 1990 and the like, are caught against adjacent records. Date pairs are forced into order, treatment start no earlier than diagnosis, outcome date no earlier than treatment start. A missing date inherits the cohort median date for its variable.

\subsubsection*{Temporal Consistency}

Every fill in the longitudinal structure is forward-only. That alone keeps the temporal data from leaking. A Month 4 observation can carry forward to Month 5; a Month 6 measurement can never inform an earlier step the model is meant to predict without it. After MICE, a monotonicity repair pass reverses any drop in \texttt{cumulative\_doses\_taken} that the multivariate imputer introduced, so a patient's cumulative dose never falls from one treatment month to the next.

\subsubsection*{Categorical Harmonization Audit}

A cardinality audit of the raw categorical features confirmed how widespread the entry inconsistency was. The \texttt{chest\_x\_ray\_at\_case\_notification} field carried over 300 unique free-text entries, \texttt{co\_morbidities} more than 150; even nominally binary fields like \texttt{sex} and \texttt{outcome} showed 5 to 12 raw representations each, the residue of treatment centers abbreviating in their own way. Those counts drove a 17-field harmonization pass that maps free-text and inconsistently coded entries onto standardized categories before analysis.

\subsubsection*{Missing Data Diagnostics and Feature Importance}

The diagnostic-first missing data pipeline, detailed in the Missing Data Handling subsection, adds one more validation layer. Little's MCAR test runs at the dataset level. Per-column mechanisms come from two correlation checks, Spearman rank to catch temporal dropout, point-biserial to separate MAR from MNAR. A Random Forest trained on available cases ranks predictive importance, and that ranking decides where each variable is routed.

\subsubsection*{Post-Processing Verification}

Before the cleaned dataset reaches any model, an automated routine checks four things: (1) the identifier columns are still present for traceability, (2) no scaled or normalized values have slipped in, so everything stays in original clinical units, (3) categorical labels are still human-readable strings, not integer codes, and (4) no one-hot columns have appeared. Pass all four, and encoding, scaling, and tensor construction were demonstrably left to the model-specific scripts that run after the split.

\subsubsection*{Leakage Prevention and Reproducibility}

Every validation step is there for one reason: keep test-set information out of training. The patient-level stratified split comes first, before any transformation. Scalers, imputers, and encoders are fit on the training set alone, then carried to the test and validation sets through \texttt{reindex()} so the column sets line up exactly. Temporal fills run forward-only, backward-fill switched off. Reproducibility is handled two ways: the split is fully deterministic under a seeded random state, and the fitted scaler parameters are checked by SHA-256 hash to confirm that independent runs preprocess identically. An audit trail, \texttt{missing\_data\_report.json}, logs the decision pathway, mechanism classification, and rationale for every column.

\subsection{Identification of Variables Associated With Treatment Outcomes}

To find which variables track with treatment outcomes, the study pairs descriptive association analysis with model-derived explanation. The descriptive side summarizes which demographic, clinical, diagnostic, and temporal variables pull the success and failure groups apart. The supervised models then estimate how those same variables combine to predict the outcome. Both are needed, because risk in TB treatment is multidimensional. A variable can look weak on its own and still matter once it sits next to adherence, diagnostic, geographic, or monitoring information.

Connecting predictive performance to clinical interpretability falls to Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), a post-hoc method that measures each variable's marginal contribution to the model's output . SHAP shows which features pushed a patient-level prediction toward ``Success'' (Cured, Completed) or ``Failure'' (Failed, Died, Lost to Follow-up; ). In clinical review, that amounts to seeing what the model leaned on, drug resistance, comorbidities, adherence gaps, diagnostic results, a delayed diagnosis. One caution holds throughout: these explanations describe how the model behaved, not clinical causality, and the line between computational attribution and medical judgment is kept deliberately clear .

\subsection{Temporal Modeling}

TB treatment runs 6 to 12 months, so the study adds temporal modeling on the longitudinal monitoring data. Train a static model on baseline features alone and it can miss how adherence, follow-up attendance, and lab measurements move over the course. The temporal models are built to give month-by-month risk estimates from treatment Month 0 (M0) through Month 12 (M12).

These models run on one dataset only, the 599-patient longitudinal set from the Data Acquisition and Preprocessing subsection (see also Table~\ref{tab:dataset_comparison}), the one the Pharmacy Co-authors compiled by hand from facility monitoring forms. It is not the 7,389-patient DOH-CAR registry the static classifiers use; the two are separate. After the eight-stage temporal pipeline, each patient has up to 8 usable monthly timesteps, M0 to M7. Months past M7 are dropped from the model inputs. Fewer than 25\% of patients had observations that far out (Table~\ref{tab:retention_stats_by_month}), and a surviving subset that small and skewed gives unreliable estimates. As before, encoding, scaling, and 3-dimensional tensor preparation wait for the post-split, model-specific scripts, so nothing leaks.

Temporal modeling went through two iterations, v1 and v2. They share the same dataset lineage; v2 is the corrected, modeling-ready preparation that came out of a methodological review of v1. The v1 pipeline was a modeling baseline, with patient-level stratified splitting and progressive temporal expansion across M0 to M12. The review then flagged a few things that could weaken its clinical defensibility: late-treatment fields that raise the risk of outcome leakage, inconsistent handling of imputed or unreliable outcome labels, and cleaning and temporal feature construction that had not been audited closely enough.

Version 2 (v2) fixed those issues and retrained. It builds on the audited output of the eight-stage cleaning pipeline from Data Acquisition and Preprocessing, restricts supervised learning to patients whose outcome is actually observed, and removes leakage-prone and administrative fields, outcome-date fields and late-timeline variables that would not exist at an early inference. Temporal predictors are encoded two ways: as sequence inputs for the deep models, as engineered summaries for the tree-based ones. The deep architecture is a Hybrid Bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM), picked to model long-term dependencies without the vanishing gradients of a plain RNN ; its recurrent structure suits the strong month-to-month adherence dependence the lag-1 analysis turned up . The tree-based baselines take the other route, flattening the monthly values and bolting on aggregates, trends, latest-observed values, and missingness indicators. Class imbalance is corrected with training-only resampling such as SMOTE-ENN. The hybrid framing is kept so temporal predictions can sit beside strong static learners like XGBoost, which holds up better across the different patient subgroups .

\subsection{Supervised Learning Model Development and Validation}

At the center of the study, supervised models are trained and validated to classify treatment outcomes. The algorithms are ensemble tree-based ones, which handle complex non-linear interactions among mixed-type clinical variables and take class imbalance in stride: XGBoost , LightGBM , and Random Forest . The three came out of a preliminary run across six candidates that also included Logistic Regression, SVM, and Gradient Boosting. The three that lost out either discriminated poorly (CV-AUC $\leq 0.63$) or recalled almost none of the minority failure class, either of which makes a model unsafe inside a CDSS. Each surviving classifier runs under two sampling strategies: no resampling with native class-weighting, and SMOTE with Edited Nearest Neighbors (SMOTE-ENN), which inflates the training failure class by 433\% to a near-balanced 0.70:1 ratio.

\subsubsection{Feature Set Versions}

Three feature sets are evaluated in sequence, to see how much each extra clinical and temporal block actually buys. The \textit{baseline} set (11 features) is the core demographic and diagnostic block: age, sex, anatomical site, bacteriologic status, microscopy result, registration group, source of patient, TB type, province, days to treatment, and year of registration. The \textit{improved} set (14 features) adds three facility-level geographic variables: city/municipality, treatment health facility, and screening/diagnosing health facility. The \textit{extended} set (21 features) adds a further seven temporal and diagnostic delay features from date engineering: diagnosis month, diagnosis quarter, diagnosis season (on the Philippine three-season meteorological calendar: \textit{Tag-lamig} for cool dry December to February, \textit{Tag-init} for hot dry March to May, \textit{Tag-ulan} for the rainy June to November), days from screening to diagnosis, days from diagnosis to microscopy result, days from diagnosis to rapid diagnostic test (RDT) result, and the RDT result itself (binned from 42 raw variants into three classes: Detected, Not Detected, Not Done). Date of outcome was kept out of every feature set. That field is generated after the treatment result, so including it would leak the outcome.

\subsubsection{Data Partitioning and Class Imbalance}

A stratified 70/20/10 patient-level split divides the data into training (70\%), test (20\%), and holdout validation (10\%), with the class proportions held across all three . The imbalance is heavy, roughly 86\% success against 14\% failure, and it is corrected on the training set only, with one of three oversamplers. SMOTE  interpolates synthetic minority-class points. SMOTE-ENN  runs SMOTE, then clears ambiguous borderline cases with Edited Nearest Neighbors undersampling. SMOTE-Tomek  runs SMOTE, then removes Tomek links to tidy the boundary. The test and validation sets are never resampled. They keep their original distributions, so the evaluation stays unbiased .

\subsubsection{Cross-Validation and Model Selection}

Every configuration is scored by five-fold stratified cross-validation on the training data, which estimates generalization and returns a mean and standard deviation of ROC-AUC across folds . The clinical goal is to catch at-risk patients early, so selection runs on a composite metric, $\text{score} = 0.6 \times \text{AUC} + 0.4 \times \text{Recall(Failure)}$: it leans on discrimination but still rewards sensitivity to the minority failure class. Whichever configuration tops that score across the feature versions and resamplers is then checked on the held-out 10\% validation set for an unbiased real-world estimate.

Each configuration also reports the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) next to the composite metric, as a supplementary check. Accuracy or a class-specific F1 can mislead; MCC reads all four quadrants of the confusion matrix at once and holds up under heavy imbalance. It sits in $[-1, +1]$: $+1$ for perfect prediction, $0$ for random, $-1$ for complete inversion . Putting MCC beside the composite score exposes the trade-off that aggressive resampling brings, since SMOTE-ENN and its kind can lift failure recall while piling on false positives that push overall error up.

\subsubsection{Performance Evaluation}

Performance is read across several metrics, each one catching a different facet of classification quality under imbalance. Treatment failure is only 13.8\% of the cohort, so leaning on a single number, accuracy most of all, can mislead. A classifier that answers ``Success'' every time scores 86.2\% accuracy and catches not one failure. The framework therefore reports discriminative, threshold-dependent, and calibration-aware metrics side by side.

Every metric traces back to the confusion matrix, with treatment failure as the positive class. On the held-out test set, each model produces four quadrants: true positives (TP, failures correctly predicted), false negatives (FN, failures missed), false positives (FP, successes wrongly flagged as failures), and true negatives (TN, successes correctly identified). The metrics below all build on these four:

\textbf{Accuracy} ($\frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}$) is the overall share of correct predictions. Intuitive, yes, but under 86:14 imbalance the majority class dominates it, so it is reported with that caveat attached.

\textbf{Precision} ($\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$), or positive predictive value, is the share of failure alerts that turn out to be real failures. When precision is low, false alarms pile up, and clinician trust in the CDSS goes with them.

\textbf{Recall} ($\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$), also sensitivity or the true positive rate, is the share of actual failures the model catches. In TB-DOTS a missed failure costs more than a false alert: an undetected treatment breakdown can run on into disease progression, drug resistance, and further community transmission.

\textbf{Specificity} ($\frac{TN}{TN + FP}$), the true negative rate, is the share of actual successes correctly identified. Keep specificity high and patients who are doing fine are not pulled in for needless review.

\textbf{F1 Score} ($2 \times \frac{Precision \times Recall}{Precision + Recall}$) is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, a single number that punishes a lopsided split between the two. It is handy for comparing models across resampling strategies.

\textbf{Balanced Accuracy} ($\frac{Recall + Specificity}{2}$) averages sensitivity and specificity, which makes it robust to class imbalance by construction. At 0.50 it is random guessing, whatever the class distribution.

\textbf{Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC)} measures discrimination across every possible decision threshold. The ROC curve plots true positive rate against false positive rate at each threshold; the AUC collapses that curve to one scalar. 1.0 is perfect separation, 0.5 is chance. Because it does not depend on a single threshold, ROC-AUC is the primary ranking metric here, though it can read optimistically under severe imbalance, where the large majority class dominates the false positive rate.

\textbf{Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (PR-AUC)} answers that limitation, plotting precision against recall across thresholds and ignoring true negatives outright. When the positive class is rare, PR-AUC tells you more than ROC-AUC . Its no-skill baseline is just the positive-class prevalence (0.138), a natural reference point.

\textbf{Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)} is computed as:
\[
MCC = \frac{TP \times TN - FP \times FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}
\]
MCC takes in all four confusion-matrix quadrants at once and stays informative under class imbalance. The coefficient runs in $[-1, +1]$: $+1$ perfect, $0$ random, $-1$ fully inverted . Unlike accuracy or F1, it is symmetric under class swapping and does not inflate when the majority class dominates.

\textbf{Threshold optimization.} Under heavy imbalance a 0.50 cutoff need not line up with clinical priorities, so each model gets a threshold search. Candidates from 0.10 to 0.90 are scored against composite objectives, each encoding a different priority: \textit{balanced} (40\% F1, 30\% recall, 30\% specificity), \textit{failure\_recall} (55\% failure recall, 25\% balanced accuracy, 20\% F1), and \textit{specificity} (50\% specificity, 30\% F1, 20\% recall). A model reports its metrics at whichever threshold maxes out the relevant objective.

\textbf{Model selection} uses a composite score weighting discriminative performance and clinical utility:
\[
S = 0.6 \times \text{ROC-AUC} + 0.4 \times \text{Recall(Failure)}
\]
Most of the weight sits on ranking ability (AUC), but the score still rewards sensitivity to the minority failure class. Whichever configuration tops it becomes the recommended model.

\textbf{Validation strategy.} Performance is estimated at three levels, to guard against overfitting and to gauge generalization. Five-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set gives the mean and standard deviation of ROC-AUC across folds, a stability check. The 20\% held-out test set carries the primary evaluation behind every metric above, on data the model never met in training or preprocessing. A final 10\% holdout is reserved for the recommended model alone, to confirm the test-set numbers are not an artifact of one split. Scaling, encoding, and imputation are all fit on the training set, then applied to the test and validation sets, so no evaluation data feeds back into training.

For reproducibility, every experiment goes through one centralized pipeline that standardizes training, cross-validation, and evaluation. Metrics and visualizations export straight to native \LaTeX{} for this manuscript.

\subsection{Development of a Clinical Decision Support Tool}

The validated models are embedded in a Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) built for healthcare providers, each part with its own job. The predictive model estimates the probability of treatment success or failure. The SHAP layer names the factors behind that estimate. The interface displays the risk and those factors, and the clinician reads the whole thing against what they already know about the patient. In practice the provider opens a dashboard: the failure and success probabilities, a risk-factor view that surfaces what drove the score (adherence gaps, lab results, diagnostic delays), and live updates. Enter new follow-up data, a monthly weight or a smear result, and the prediction refreshes, so review carries on across the whole treatment. The CDSS is for risk stratification and explanation. It does not diagnose or prescribe on its own.

\subsection{Small Language Model (SLM)-Based Narrative Explanation Layer}

To make the explanations easier on clinical users, the CDSS adds a small language model (SLM), a constrained narrative layer that renders model outputs as plain clinical text. The SHAP feature contributions remain the explanation of record. The SLM is a presentation layer and nothing more, not a predictor, not a diagnostic engine, not a source of fresh clinical inference . It runs after prediction and touches nothing upstream: not the training, not the inference probabilities, not the risk thresholds, not the SHAP values. In the implemented pipeline, the web client assembles a structured interpretation request, selected patient context fields, the predicted treatment-failure probability, and the ranked contribution signals, then posts it to a dedicated endpoint that streams the narrative back into the interface.

The SLM stays confined to explanation. It does not infer new clinical causes, write prescriptions, suggest regimen changes, or stand in for a clinician's judgment. Its one job is to make the SHAP-grounded attribution easier to read, so a provider can see why the CDSS flagged a patient and decide whether a closer look is warranted. Keeping the roles this separate matches how medical-domain SLMs are framed today, and how health AI governance expects bounded decision support to behave, and it keeps interpretability tied to the original attribution signal . The final call still belongs to the treating professional.

\subsection{Missing Data Handling Outcomes}

Of the 44 features with missing data, 15 went out by column exclusion, every one of them past the 80\% hard threshold. The near-empty ones sat at the top: tuberculosis culture (99.8\%), other laboratory tests (99.8\%), tuberculin skin test (93.5\%). A sixteenth, the raw blood pressure string field, was soft-excluded at 67.3\% missing and below-median importance, since it had already been parsed into structured systolic and diastolic fields. No rows were dropped at all; all 599 patient records stayed in. That full retention matters with a cohort this small and an imbalance this steep, 86.2\% success against 13.8\% failure, where listwise deletion would only have thinned the minority class further .

Listwise deletion never fired. The dataset-level MCAR test was rejected, and no single variable met all three of its conditions at once, confirmed MCAR, under 15\% missing, and over 500 patients left after deletion. Twenty-five variables took the missing-indicator-with-fill route instead: eight temporal monthly features (weight, percentage adherence, cumulative doses taken, monthly smear results among them) and 17 static baseline variables with confirmed MNAR patterns. Only four, oxygen saturation, heart rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, went to stochastic MICE, each confirmed MAR. Their missingness indicators tracked visit attendance under the point-biserial procedure above, which is the clinically sensible reading: whether a vital sign was recorded depended on whether the patient showed up that month, not on the measurement's own unseen value. Reserving the expensive probabilistic imputation for just these four reflects the diagnostic-first design, which only spends MICE where its assumptions actually hold .

\subsection{Longitudinal Trends (2015--2025)}

Annual treatment outcomes from 2015 to 2025 moved with both program changes and external shocks. Treatment success held fairly steady in the low-to-mid 80\% range across most of the decade, dipping to 81.03\% in 2018 before climbing to a peak of 88.92\% in 2023.

Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} gives the full metric breakdown for all six configurations. Models were ranked by a composite clinical score, $S = 0.6 \times \text{AUC} + 0.4 \times \text{Recall\,(Failure)}$, which weights discriminative ability against sensitivity to treatment failure. Failure is the outcome a CDSS alert exists to catch.

XGBoost with SMOTE-ENN was the recommended model ($S = 0.699$). Its failure-class recall, 72.9\%, was the highest of any configuration, so it caught nearly three-quarters of the patients who went on to fail treatment. Overall accuracy was lower (55.5\%) because SMOTE-ENN resamples the training set aggressively, and its test-set AUC was moderate (0.680), but the trade is clinically worth it: in a TB CDSS a missed failure (a false negative) means an undetected treatment breakdown, with the risk of disease progression or drug resistance, whereas a false alert only triggers an extra clinical review. The model's 5-fold CV AUC of $0.686 \pm 0.015$ held steady across folds, and holdout validation returned 68.2\% recall, so the recall advantage carries to unseen data.

Among the models without resampling, XGBoost (no sampling) had the highest test-set AUC (0.712) and CV-AUC ($0.702 \pm 0.009$) of all six configurations, with a competitive 56.6\% failure recall. It is the better pick when the goal is a continuous, well-calibrated risk score, for instance to rank patients by risk rather than apply a binary alert threshold. LightGBM (no sampling) was close behind ($\text{AUC} = 0.708$, recall $= 55.2\%$) and corroborates it. The three configurations left out were Random Forest under both samplers and LightGBM with SMOTE-ENN; each scored lower on the composite, dragged down by weak failure recall relative to its AUC.

The MCC values in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} show what the recall-focused choice costs. XGBoost with SMOTE-ENN scored an MCC of 0.175, the lowest of the six test-set configurations, while XGBoost without resampling scored the highest (0.258) and so balanced the four confusion-matrix quadrants best. The gap is the familiar tension in imbalanced clinical classification: SMOTE-ENN lifts failure recall by adding a large volume of synthetic minority-class samples in training, but the trained model then throws proportionally more false positives on the real test distribution, and MCC penalizes that symmetrically. So the SMOTE-ENN model fits a screening deployment where catching every at-risk patient matters more than avoiding false alerts, and the no-resampling model fits the case where the cost of unnecessary reviews is the binding constraint.

Adherence is stable month to month in the temporal dataset, and that is the empirical case for the temporal architecture. \section{Discussion}

This study looked at machine learning models for predicting treatment outcomes (Success or Failure) among PTB patients in the DOH-CAR TB-DOTS program, using monthly monitoring data together with baseline clinical features. Temporal modeling went through two iterations. The v1 iteration was an initial baseline, and its results show a common trap in imbalanced clinical prediction: a model can post high accuracy by siding with the majority Success class and still miss the real treatment failures. Its specificity was 0.0000 on the held-out test split, so Failure identification was not reliable under that first workflow.

The corrected v2 iteration improved the methodological defensibility of temporal prediction by addressing leakage risk, outcome-label handling, and temporal feature preparation. On the v2 held-out test split, the SMOTE-ENN tree models achieved consistent performance and identified 5 of 6 Failure cases while correctly classifying 36 of 38 Success cases. Random Forest had the strongest held-out ranking among the tree models (ROC-AUC 0.9693, PR-AUC 0.9950), while LightGBM reported the strongest cross-validated balanced accuracy in the saved artifacts. The v2 Hybrid Bi-LSTM baseline detected all Failure cases on the held-out test split (specificity 1.0000) but raised more false Failure alerts among true Success cases, which lowered overall accuracy. This tradeoff can be acceptable in a screening-oriented CDSS where missing high-risk patients is more costly than additional review workload, but it must be managed to avoid over-alerting.

The study makes a few contributions. Pairing temporal monitoring data with static baseline features showed that patient-level longitudinal information predicts better than baseline data alone. Using SMOTE-ENN and the Bi-LSTM data augmentation against the extreme class imbalance in TB outcomes gave the models a better shot at catching high-risk patients. The work also shows why prediction has to come with explanation in a clinical setting: a risk estimate is easier to inspect once providers can see the patient-level factors behind the alert. SHAP explanations and bounded SLM summaries are that interpretability layer. They tie the model output to clinician review without taking the decision out of the clinician's hands.

The v2 temporal outputs look like a reasonable basis for further evaluation inside TB monitoring workflows, mainly for spotting earlier the patients who need closer review. For deployment, the v2 tree models (Random Forest and LightGBM with SMOTE-ENN especially) strike a good balance between Failure detection and overall correctness, while the Hybrid Bi-LSTM baseline suits a more cautious screening stance. Any clinical or programmatic use should first go through external validation, prospective testing, and review by healthcare professionals to check safety, calibration, and workflow fit. Future work should move to larger, multi-region datasets for better generalization, track temporal calibration over time, and test hybrids that combine deep temporal representations with tree-based learners. Adding interpretability over the temporal dynamics, such as attention across treatment months, may also help clinician trust and adoption.

Together these CDSS artifacts move the program from retrospective reporting toward earlier clinical management. NTP protocols usually catch treatment failure only after it happens; the predictive outputs instead allow intervention during the early months of therapy, when it still helps. The \textit{Full Breakdown} report works against the black-box character of ensemble models, since it lets health workers check the model's logic against their own clinical experience. The \textit{First Step} document then carries that accuracy into the care pathway, which can cut the MDR-TB that unrecognized non-adherence would otherwise produce. Pairing SHAP with Med-Gemma also speaks to the usual skepticism about AI in healthcare: with both a statistical breakdown and a written narrative, the CDSS keeps a human in the loop, letting DOH-CAR health workers weigh the model's findings against their own judgment before acting.

\subsection{Recommendations}

Healthcare facilities and policymakers should consider adding tree-based models (Random Forest and LightGBM with SMOTE-ENN especially) to the existing DOH-CAR TB-DOTS monitoring programs as a decision-support layer for early risk stratification. On the held-out test set these models detected treatment failure in a balanced way and still kept overall accuracy high, which makes them a practical way to flag patients who may need closer clinical review. Before any deployment they should be validated externally on independent multi-facility data, tested prospectively under real workflow conditions, and reviewed by healthcare professionals for calibration, safety, and fit with current practice.

The explainability layer needs formal evaluation before any clinical deployment. A preliminary internal check on the 44-patient test cohort is telling: stripped of the attribution signal, the narrative layer almost never reproduced the model's SHAP attribution, and not one case passed the faithfulness criterion under that blind condition (0 of 44), whichever SLM backend was used. Hand it the SHAP contribution signals in the prompt, though, and faithfulness rose sharply, which confirms the layer for what it is, a constrained verbalizer of the model's reasoning, not a reasoner of its own. The recommendation that follows is to widen the faithfulness evaluation to a larger, multi-facility cohort, run several SLM backends, and add clinician-rated readability, trust, and actionability as paired outcome measures, building the evidence base a safe deployment of the explanation component would require.

\subsection{Safety Scope and Interpretation Limits}

The explanation layer exists to make the model's reasoning readable, turning contribution signals into text a clinician can follow, but its output has to be read inside firm safety bounds. SHAP says how a feature pushed the predicted risk up or down; it does not establish a causal mechanism of disease progression, nor does it promise that changing a highlighted factor will change the patient's outcome. That distinction earns its weight in high-stakes settings, where a generative output is easy to over-read past what actually supports it .

The system is therefore positioned as decision support: it flags risk, surfaces the contributing factors, and summarizes how the model behaved, while diagnosis and treatment decisions stay with the clinician. That framing lines up with current reporting and governance guidance for AI-assisted clinical prediction, where transparency, bounded use, and human oversight are still the core requirements .

The preliminary faithfulness evaluation described above supports this bounded framing by showing that the narrative layer depends on SHAP contribution signals rather than reconstructing model attribution from patient context alone. Accordingly, the operational design retains SHAP as the primary explanation surface and treats the narrative output as a readability aid rather than a substitute for the attribution itself.

\section{Conclusion}

TB treatment in the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) runs into very different patient situations, patchy record-keeping, and shocks to the health program. One-variable analyses cannot fully account for that. So the study started by building a clear, repeatable cleaning pipeline for two related datasets, a 7{,}389-patient DOH-CAR registry and a 599-patient month-by-month monitoring set. The pipeline standardized formats, stripped patient identifiers, and decided each missing value by first asking why it was missing, MCAR, MAR, or MNAR, before sending it down one of four matching strategies. With the cleaned data trustworthy instead of blindly filled, the descriptive and exploratory work could show what mattered: outcome trends across the years, the rise in deaths and lost follow-ups through the COVID-19 period, and the patient-level, time-based, and operational patterns that later shaped the models.

Those cleaned signals carried straight into model performance. On the static dataset, XGBoost with SMOTE-ENN was the strongest screening setup (composite score $S = 0.699$; failure recall 72.9\%), while XGBoost without resampling gave the best-calibrated risk ranking (test AUC 0.712; MCC 0.258), two setups for two different uses. The longitudinal models went further. Once the v2 pipeline fixed the leakage and label-handling problems that had held v1 back, the SMOTE-ENN tree models (Random Forest, LightGBM, XGBoost) hit 0.9318 accuracy and 0.8333 specificity, catching five of six failure cases on the held-out test set; the Hybrid Bi-LSTM caught all of them but raised more false alarms. On top of these predictions sit SHAP feature explanations and a bounded SLM narrative layer, pulled together into a Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) that shows each patient's risk, the factors behind it, and a refreshed estimate as new monthly data arrives.

The results are promising enough clinically, but not ready for real clinical use. The test cases came from a single regional registry, and the narrative layer's faithfulness was checked on just 44 patients, so the system still has to clear external validation on data from other facilities, prospective testing in real workflows, and review by healthcare professionals for safety, calibration, and fit. The narrative layer in particular needs a larger faithfulness study before it goes anywhere near practice. Only once those conditions are met can the framework, and the published pseudonymized derivative of the DOH-CAR PTB Treatment Outcome Dataset, support regional TB control and further work on explainable clinical prediction.
