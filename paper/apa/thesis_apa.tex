\textbf{Abstract}

% APA 7th: Abstract is a single unindented paragraph
{
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
Pulmonary Tuberculosis (PTB) is still lingers as a big public health concern in the Cordilleras, where differences in patients and gaps in the treatment program still contributes to treatment failure. This study develops and evaluates machine learning models to predict treatment success or failure among patients in DOH-CAR TB-DOTS facilities for the years from 2015 to 2025. A preprocessing pipeline performs the following: schema harmonization, patient pseudonymization (for anonymization), and a diagnostic-first missing data handling protocol. The protocol classifies each variable's missingness mechanism as Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), Missing at Random (MAR), or Missing Not at Random (MNAR), then routes the variable to one of four strategies: column exclusion, listwise deletion, missing indicator imputation, or stochastic Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). Supervised tree-based classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, and Random Forest) are trained alongside Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) temporal architectures to model longitudinal treatment dynamics over the 6- to 12-month treatment course. Because clinical decision support needs more than an accurate risk score, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) are used to show which patient-level factors contributed to each prediction so that clinicians can review the system's outputs. The validated models are embedded in a Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) that provides dynamic risk estimates, SHAP-grounded factor explanations, and bounded narrative summaries. The CDSS is intended to support early clinical intervention while preserving clinician judgment.

Pulmonary Tuberculosis (PTB) is a chronic infectious disease caused by \textit{Mycobacterium tuberculosis} (MTB), which is a slow-growing, acid-fast bacillus. And because the disease progresses gradually and that treatment can fail, mitigation is difficult \citep{ref20}. Effective treatments exist, but PTB still is widespread. It tracks closely with poverty, which is naturally followed by undernutrition, and comorbidities as well such as diabetes and HIV, all of which weaken patients' immune defenses and raise susceptibility to the disease \citep{ref20}. These conditions also delay the diagnosis and complicate treatment. TB is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, and in 2018 alone, there were around 10 million new cases and 1.5 million deaths, and about half a million of those were resistant to rifampicin.

In the Philippines, TB still remains as a big problem with regards to public health, and the World Health Organization (WHO) lists the country as one of the biggest contributors to the global TB burden, estimated at around 6.8 percent of cases coming from the Philippines. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, Philippine TB rates were also among the highest in Asia: over 554 cases per 100,000 people in 2019 \citep{ref8}. In 2023, about 37,000 Filipinos died of TB out of 739,000 diagnosed cases \citep{who2023tb}, and the WHO classifies the country as a high-burden setting for both MDR-TB and TB/HIV co-infection \citep{ref18}. The DOH recorded 575,770 TB cases that year through its Integrated TB Information System, a figure that points to substantial under-registration and underdiagnosis \citep{ref4}. The DOH applies CDC-recommended regimens for active and latent TB through the TB-DOTS strategy, which was expanded in 2003 to cover private clinics, pharmacies, and hospitals \citep{ref5, ref6}. Gaps in diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and patient support nonetheless remain, particularly in the management of MDR-TB \citep{ref29}.

In Baguio City, the DOH-CAR reported 437 confirmed TB cases in 2022 along with over 4,405 missing or unreported cases, making the city a priority for surveillance and treatment improvement \citep{ref25}. The gap is driven by passive case-finding limitations, stigma, and financial barriers that let undetected patients keep transmitting the disease within their communities \citep{ref26}.

The large population and a lot of daily traffic (both foot and vehicular) in the city may increase opportunities for tubercolosis to spread. The colder climate can also create more ideal conditions for the transmission of \textit{Mycobacterium tuberculosis}. The city also has a more inflated local load of cases because it takes in more complex tuberculosis cases from neighboring provinces because of it being a tertiary referral center \citep{ref30}. Patient-level factors also play an important role in treatment outcomes such as comorbidities like diabetes mellitus, HIV infection, and malnutrition, all of which weaken their immune response and may reduce treatment completion. Food insecurity, poor housing conditions, and transportation costs, can also interrupt adherence to directly observed treatment short-course (DOTS). \citep{ref30,ref32,ref33,ref34}.

All in all, these constraints argue for strategies that work on more than one facet of assessment. Treatment failure not only worsens the outcome for the singular patient, it also extends the period for which transmission can happen in the community and in-turn speeds up the emergence of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), defined as resistance to at least rifampicin and isoniazid. MDR-TB requires regimens that are longer, more toxic, and more expensive, and it carries much lower success rates. The result is a wider threat to regional disease control in the Philippines \citep{ref28,ref35,ref36}.
\subsection{Risk Factors for Treatment Failure}

To ensure consistent evaluation of tuberculosis control efforts, the Department of Health (DOH) established standardized criteria for classifying treatment outcomes through the National Tuberculosis Program (NTP). These outcome definitions are important for monitoring program performance, comparing findings across studies, and identifying factors associated with treatment outcomes. Treatment success and failure do not occur randomly, as they are often influenced by identifiable patient, clinical, and program-related risk factors. Mortality remains the primary outcome examined in most studies on treatment failure, although some also include disease progression, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, and loss to follow-up as indicators of poor treatment outcomes \citep{ref6}.

One of the risk factors is close or direct contact with patients who have active TB infection where patients who acquired the infection through close contact were at higher risk of delayed diagnosis and more likely to be non-adherent, either by refusing or quitting the regimen \citep{ref7}. Another risk factor is recurrence of the infection or a history of receiving TB treatment because patients who missed follow-ups had a higher risk of reactivation and mortality, and were harder to treat than patients with no prior history of TB \citep{ref21}. Frequent missed or interrupted treatment also leads to drug resistance \citep{ref13}.

Comorbidities have consistently shown a significant influence on tuberculosis treatment outcomes across different settings. In Eastern Ethiopia, HIV co-infection was a substantial risk factor for treatment failure because the weakened immune system makes treatment less effective \citep{ref2}. TB and HIV drug interactions also accelerate each other's progression and increase the occurrence of side effects, which can compromise treatment success \citep{ref3}. A study in Davao City, found a higher risk of treatment failure in comorbid patients: 58.82\% of the 51 patients who experienced treatment failure had at least one comorbidity \citep{ref9}. Another major factor is Diabetes Mellitus (DM). TB patients with diagnosed DM have a higher risk of treatment failure, relapse, and death than non-diabetics \citep{ref14}. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, DM patients had a higher risk of treatment failure for Drug-resistant (DR) TB and mortality for MDR-TB, with odds ratios of 1.56 and 1.57 respectively \citep{ref22}. Another study reported that patients with both PTB and chronic conditions, especially DM, had reduced rates of sputum conversion and a higher probability of poor treatment outcomes, including death and progression to MDR-TB \citep{ref17}.

These findings show that TB treatment failure cannot be driven by any single factor, it comes from how comorbidities, programmatic gaps, and socioeconomic vulnerabilities interact. Conventional single-factor analyses do not capture this. An integrative machine learning framework that can model these multidimensional interactions is therefore needed to enable earlier and more targeted clinical intervention \citep{ref2, ref9, ref14, ref15, ref16, ref22, ref25, ref28, ref30}.

\subsection{Machine Learning for TB Outcome Prediction}

Previous studies on tuberculosis treatment outcomes have focused on sociodemographic characteristics, comorbidities, medical history, and other patient-level risk factors. The findings remain inconsistent and fragmented because methodological constraints limit how these factors are analyzed together. Most studies rely on traditional statistical analyses such as statistical tests or regression models, which examine one factor at a time, or a limited set of factors, while assuming linear relationships among variables \citep{ref2}. These approaches provide important evidence on individual risk factors, but they may miss the combined and non-linear effects that shape treatment success or failure.

Algorithms that learn from high-dimensional data can pick up subtle dependencies that affect whether treatment succeeds or fails, and this is where machine learning (ML) has an edge over conventional statistical analysis. The interactions among demographic, clinical, diagnostic, and treatment-related variables are complex and non-linear, and those are exactly the patterns single-factor methods are most likely to miss. Logistic regression, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and ensemble methods like Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) have all been applied to this kind of problem \citep{ref16}. In TB specifically, earlier work has used ML to predict drug resistance, flag high-risk patients, and optimize treatment strategies \citep{ref26, ref27}. What this shows is that predictive models can take risk factor analysis further by pulling together several dimensions of patient data at once.

Interpretability is a must with clinical decision support systems, especially when dealing with outputs from ML models that only show the end result without the grounds for why the output is the way it is. Practitioners in healthcare must be able to grasp the basis of a risk alert before they act on it. A model that assigns high treatment-failure risk that doesn't flag the contributing factors is hard for TB-DOTS staff to trust on and decide for follow-up actions. To mitigate this need, the study thereby uses Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), a post-hoc technique using Shapley values and does by quantifying each variable/factor's contribution to a prediction and can summarize both patient-level explanations and overall feature importance \citep{lundberg2017unified,lundberg2020local}. This is useful for tree-based clinical models such as XGBoost, Random Forest, and LightGBM because predictive performance may be high but the internal decision structure is hard to check. Healthcare ML explainability in literature mention SHAP as one of the most used methods for explaining prediction models, especially for tabular data \citep{allgaier2023model}. Studies that focused on explainability of clinical ML models used SHAP to identify meaningful contributors to treatment failure, including drug resistance, TB history, lab markers, and other factors \citep{ref15,wang2025biochemical,sibanda2026explainable}.

Fragmented risk factor studies have clear limits, and interpretable ML offers a way past them: analyzing what drives TB treatment outcomes calls for combining several dimensions of patient data rather than examining them one at a time. The present study uses a multi-layer framework over demographic, clinical, diagnostic, and treatment-related variables. Descriptive analysis characterizes patient profiles and treatment patterns, supervised models estimate treatment outcome risk, and SHAP-based explanations show which factors drive each risk estimate. This improves predictive performance while keeping the outputs transparent for healthcare providers \citep{ref23, ref24}, helping them spot patients at higher risk of failure, see the factors behind a given estimate, and act through early intervention, closer monitoring, and better patient management.

\subsection{Contributions}

The DOH-CAR TB-DOTS program follows the National Tuberculosis Program (NTP) protocol. Case finding is passive and starts at the facility. People with TB symptoms such as a long-lasting cough, weight loss, and fever are sent for testing, including sputum smear microscopy, the GeneXpert MTB/RIF test, and chest X-ray \citep{ref5, ref6}. Once diagnosed, a patient is recorded in the Treatment Card and Master List, given a registration group, and assigned to a health worker or trained treatment partner who watches them take each dose over the six- to twelve-month course \citep{ref6}. Monthly visits track sputum results, record vital signs and weight, and check whether the patient is keeping up with treatment. Outcomes are later classed under NTP criteria as Cured, Treatment Completed, Failed, Died, or Lost to Follow-up \citep{ref19}. The process is mostly paper-based, and risk is judged in hindsight. Problems such as a worsening condition, missed doses, or complications from other illnesses usually surface only after they have already affected treatment, not before. High-risk patients are not flagged in time for early action, which leads to avoidable treatment failures, more deaths, and continued spread of drug-resistant (MDR) TB in the region \citep{ref28, ref30}.

The research presents six major contributions. The first contribution is developing and validating an Integrated Predictive Model utilizing machine learning models that combine training sets based upon demographic, clinical, diagnostic and treatment related variables to forecast therapy success or failure of PTB patients in CAR. Therefore giving clinicians the opportunity to identify potential problem patients earlier.

Secondly, a reproducible Data Preprocessing \& Harmonization Pipeline which takes raw patient information from TB-DOTS clinics throughout the CAR and transforms the datasets for machine learning algorithms' use, using a variety of techniques such as schema normalization; pseudonymous identification of individual patients; automatic correction of errors; and a diagnostic-first approach to handle missing values.

Thirdly, a Descriptive/Exploratory Analysis that describes the study population; provides insight into how the various attributes relate to one another; and identifies the most important predictors of treatment outcomes.

Fourthly, Temporal Modeling to examine time dependent relationships in the monthly monitoring data using Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) architectures and therefore capture variations that occur over time during treatment. Therefore, temporal modeling is expected to generate predictive accuracy that improves upon prior studies which did not account for temporal changes in monitoring data.

Fifthly, Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) were used to measure the degree to which each patient-specific factor contributed to a prediction. SHAP are also useful because they allow clinicians to see what specific factors led to each predicted risk's score and thus help evaluate whether or not a particular recommendation should be implemented for each patient.

Lastly, the validated model was embedded into a Clinical Decision Support system (CDSS) that displays a patient's current level of risk; lists all the factors that contribute to each risk assessment via SHAP; and updates a patient's risk estimate when new follow-up data becomes available.

\subsection{Research Objectives}

This study aims to develop and validate machine learning models that predict treatment success or failure among TB patients in the CAR, Philippines. Combining demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables supports early identification of patients at risk of unsuccessful treatment and provides evidence-based tools for improving TB program outcomes. The broader goal is to enable personalized clinical interventions and efficient allocation of healthcare resources. The research aims to achieve the following objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To document and pre-process patient records of PTB cases from TB-DOTS facilities in the CAR through data cleaning, feature engineering, and reporting, so that the dataset is ready for ML applications.
    \item To describe the demographic, clinical, diagnostic, and treatment characteristics of PTB patients in the dataset and provide an overview of the study population.
    \item To identify variables that influence treatment outcomes over the course of therapy, so that the most important factors affecting patient recovery can be recognized and used to guide treatment adjustments.
    \item To add temporal modeling that analyzes time-dependent patterns in patient data, so that changes and trends throughout treatment can be detected, improving the accuracy of outcome predictions and supporting timely clinical interventions.
    \item To train and validate supervised learning models for predicting treatment success or failure among PTB patients, so that individuals at risk of poor outcomes can be identified early, while the model maintains high predictive accuracy and reliability when applied to incoming TB cases.
    \item To develop a decision support tool that visualizes patient risk levels and predictive outputs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}

The dataset includes PTB patients from 2015 to 2025 in the CAR, specifically Baguio City, and excludes those still undergoing treatment at the time of data extraction. Only patients with a defined treatment outcome of either success or failure are included. The data covers demographic information, laboratory tests, diagnostic data, clinical data, treatment regimen, follow-up examinations, drug administration data, and treatment outcomes. The study has limitations tied to the sufficiency and quality of the dataset. The data is sourced from existing records, so completeness, consistency, and representativeness may be limited by incomplete clinical parameters. This may affect which risk factors can be included and weaken the analysis. The study also may not fully represent the current TB patient population, since patients currently undergoing treatment are excluded. Difficulties are also expected during data extraction from DOH-CAR. Record-keeping over the 10-year period from 2015 to 2025 may be inconsistent due to changes in the healthcare system and shifts in documentation standards. Reliance on self-reported indicators may also introduce bias and inconsistencies that affect the predictive capabilities of the model.

\subsection{Significance of the Study}

The resulting models are designed to help healthcare providers identify patients at higher risk of treatment failure, which allows earlier review, tailored monitoring, and improved patient management. Because clinical adoption depends on whether users can understand and evaluate model outputs, the framework emphasizes explainable prediction rather than risk scoring alone. SHAP-based explanations surface the patient-level factors behind each risk estimate, and the CDSS presents these factors in a form that supports clinician interpretation and programmatic decision-making. This machine learning framework supports the broader objective of strengthening TB control efforts in the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) by turning existing patient data into predictive, explainable, and actionable tools for evidence-based public health practice. The results are intended to help improve patient management and treatment planning, strengthen program implementation, and guide public health decision-making to raise the TB treatment success rate in CAR. The study also aims to maximize benefits for the public by informing health administrators about the model, which may be developed further into a solution or application to aid the local TB-DOTS program. A processed and pseudonymized derivative of the DOH-CAR PTB Treatment Outcome Dataset produced by the study will also be published to support clinical, epidemiological, and machine learning research.

\section{Method}

This study uses PTB patient records from the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR), Philippines, covering 2015 to 2025 and consolidated by the Department of Health-CAR (DOH-CAR). Once ethical approval was granted, records were pseudonymized and run through a standardized preprocessing pipeline (schema harmonization, error correction, feature scaling) and then the diagnostic-first missing data protocol described in the Missing Data Handling subsection below. Exploratory and cluster analyses characterize the cohort and yield subgroup-level features. Ensemble tree-based classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest) are then trained to predict treatment outcomes defined by National Tuberculosis Program criteria. Because treatment unfolds over months, we also evaluate temporal models built on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) alongside the static ones, with ensembling used to improve robustness. Performance is measured with standard classification metrics, and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) handle interpretability. The final models feed a clinical decision support framework that updates risk estimates as treatment progresses. Figure~\ref{fig:data_management} gives the overview.

\subsection{Data Acquisition and Preprocessing}

Two datasets from CAR feed this study, both spanning 2015 to 2025. The first is a non-temporal (static) consolidated registry maintained by the Department of Health-CAR, which trains the static tree-based classifiers. The second is a temporal monitoring dataset that the study's Pharmacy Co-authors compiled by hand, which trains the longitudinal deep learning and temporal tree-based models. Table~\ref{tab:dataset_comparison} sets them side by side.

\subsubsection*{Non-Temporal Dataset}

DOH-CAR consolidates this dataset centrally, pulling reports from government TB-DOTS facilities and from private practitioners under the Public-Private Mix DOTS (PPMD) scheme \citep{ref6}. It holds 7,389 patient records with baseline features only: demographics (age, sex, civil status), clinical indicators (BMI, blood pressure), and diagnostic results (GeneXpert, Smear Microscopy). Each record is a single patient captured at treatment registration, with no follow-up monitoring. It is the main training source for the static classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest).

The non-temporal pipeline harmonizes variable formats and units and pseudonymizes records for privacy. It then defines the binary target from standardized National Tuberculosis Program (NTP) criteria: patients marked `Cured' or `Treatment Completed' become `Success', and those marked `Failed', `Died', or `Lost to Follow-up' become `Failure'. Extreme outliers in continuous clinical variables are capped so they do not distort training. Missing data goes through the diagnostic-first protocol described in the Missing Data Handling subsection below.

The dataset is split into training and testing sets before any transformation, which keeps test information out of the model. A stratified split holds the success and failure proportions steady across both partitions. Everything that comes after, feature scaling, normalization, and the multivariate imputation of missing values, is fit on the training set alone. Those fitted parameters and imputation rules are then applied to the held-out test set, so no model ever sees the statistical distribution of the test data.

\subsubsection*{Temporal Monitoring Dataset}

The temporal monitoring dataset is a longitudinal subset of 599 patients with complete month-by-month records. Nothing like it sat in the DOH-CAR registry. The study's Pharmacy Co-authors \textit{gathered and encoded it by hand} from paper Treatment Cards and Master Lists at TB-DOTS centers in Baguio City, Benguet. They accessed the facility records in person and transcribed each patient's monitoring data: demographics, monthly vital signs, percentage adherence, doses taken and missed, smear microscopy (TB-LAMP) and Xpert MTB/RIF results, and treatment outcomes. These went into a master \texttt{RAW DATA.xlsx} workbook with one sheet per barangay or facility unit, covering 35 facility-year cycles from 2015 to 2025. A consolidation script (\texttt{combine\_raw\_dataset.py}) then merged the per-barangay sheets into one dataset, normalizing facility names against a canonical alias map so inconsistent entries across sources lined up before the main pipeline ran.

The dataset is wide: one row per patient, with columns encoding monthly observations from treatment Month 0 (M0) through Month 12 (M12). Each monthly visit tracks percentage adherence (\texttt{pct\_adherence}), doses taken and missed that month, cumulative doses taken, body weight, height, smear microscopy (TB-LAMP), and Xpert MTB/RIF result. Each record also carries static baseline features alongside those monthly columns: demographics, baseline vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, temperature, respiratory rate), comorbidities, starting treatment regimen, and diagnostic classification. After preprocessing, the cleaned dataset is 599 rows by 269 columns.

The temporal pipeline runs in eight stages, built around the wide longitudinal layout of this dataset:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Schema Validation \& Column Standardization.} All column names are standardized to \texttt{snake\_case}; duplicate and entirely null columns are flagged and deduplicated.
  \item \textbf{Identifier Removal.} Four patient- and facility-identifying columns (\texttt{no}, \texttt{source\_file}, \texttt{name\_of\_diagnosing\_facility}, \texttt{name\_of\_treatment\_unit}) are retained in the export file for traceability but are explicitly excluded from all model inputs to enforce patient privacy.
  \item \textbf{Data Cleaning \& Type Coercion.} All columns are parsed to correct data types with clinical validation. Specialized parsers handle domain-specific formats: weight strings (\texttt{45kg}, \texttt{56.5 kg}) are converted to numeric kilograms; height strings are parsed to centimeters; blood pressure entries in \texttt{120/80} format are split into separate systolic and diastolic fields; oxygen saturation values in decimal-fraction (0.95), percent-suffix (\texttt{95\%}), and raw-integer formats are unified to a 0 to 100 scale; and adherence percentages are stripped of trailing \texttt{\%} signs. Date fields undergo a dedicated correction pass: future dates of birth are corrected (e.g., year 2063 \textrightarrow{} 1963), transposed-digit errors are detected (e.g., 2990 \textrightarrow{} 1990), and chronological ordering is enforced (treatment start $\geq$ diagnosis date). All continuous clinical variables are winsorized to physiologically plausible bounds. Temperature is clipped to 30 to 45$^\circ$C, systolic BP to 60 to 220 mmHg, diastolic BP to 30 to 140 mmHg, heart rate to 20 to 250 bpm, oxygen saturation to 70 to 100\%, weight to 10 to 180 kg, height to 80 to 220 cm, and respiratory rate to 4 to 60 breaths/min.
  \item \textbf{Categorical Harmonization.} Inconsistent categorical entries are mapped to canonical labels. Outcome variants, sex designations, regimen types, comorbidity flags, and civil status are standardized to a fixed set of valid categories, reducing per-field cardinality from hundreds of free-text variants to the expected small canonical sets.
  \item \textbf{Drop Uninformative Columns.} Near-entirely null or redundant columns (e.g., \texttt{nationality\_raw} after merging into \texttt{nationality}) are flagged in the output rather than silently deleted, maintaining auditability of the cleaning decisions.
  \item \textbf{Wide \textrightarrow{} Long Temporal Restructuring.} The wide-format dataset is melted into two structures: a static baseline table (\texttt{df\_static}, one row per patient) and a temporal table (\texttt{df\_temporal}, one row per patient-month), with columns \texttt{[patient\_id, month, weight, height, pct\_adherence, monthly\_doses\_taken, monthly\_missed\_doses, cumulative\_doses\_taken, smear\_tb\_lamp, xpert\_mtb\_rif]}.
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic-First Missing Data Handling.} The same four-pathway diagnostic pipeline described in the Missing Data Handling subsection is applied independently to both the static and temporal feature sets. Temporal features are never hard-excluded on sparsity grounds; their structural missingness (later months having fewer observations as patients complete or abandon treatment) is preserved via missingness indicators.
  \item \textbf{Post-Imputation Clinical Clipping \& Export.} Clinical bounds are re-enforced on all imputed values (e.g., weight to 10 to 180 kg, pct\_adherence to 0 to 100). A monotonicity repair pass corrects any MICE-introduced decreases in \texttt{cumulative\_doses\_taken} per patient. The pipeline exports \texttt{cleaned\_human\_readable.csv} (599 rows $\times$ 269 columns) with all original clinical units and categorical labels intact. Encoding, scaling, and tensor preparation are deliberately deferred to model-specific scripts post-train-test split to prevent data leakage.
\end{enumerate}

As in the non-temporal case, the temporal train-test split happens before any scaling, one-hot encoding, outlier capping, or 3-dimensional tensor construction for the recurrent networks. Both pipelines thus guard against leakage the same way, whether the model is a static classifier or a temporal network. \subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis}

Before any modeling, an exploratory analysis profiled the study population, checked how complete the data was, and measured the associations among variables. Its findings shaped the preprocessing and modeling decisions that followed.

\input{tables/missing_data_summary.tex}

A global missingness audit across all 28 columns mapped the dataset's sparsity (Table~\ref{tab:missing_data_summary}), ranking each column by its missing rate. Several variables were heavily incomplete: microscopy result at 37.8\%, diagnosis-derived temporal features at 53.6\%, and clinical delay intervals anywhere from 59.3\% up to 98.8\%. These figures set the missingness thresholds used later in the Missing Data Handling subsection.

Pairwise Pearson's r on available-case observations covered the continuous variables: age, four derived clinical delay intervals (days from screening to diagnosis, to treatment, to microscopy result, and to RDT result), and diagnosis day-of-year. The point was to decide whether MICE was worth using. When continuous predictors share real correlation structure, MICE can borrow signal from the observed features to estimate what's missing; when they don't, a simple univariate mean-fill does just as well.

For categorical variables, association strength came from Cram\'{e}r's V, a chi-square-based measure scaled to the \([0, 1]\) interval. Columns with fewer than two unique non-null values, or fewer than ten non-null observations, were dropped from the matrix. The categorical set covered patient demographics (sex, type of TB, anatomical site) and clinical classifications (registration group, bacteriologic status, microscopy results).

The exploratory phase also clusters patients across demographic, clinical, and diagnostic dimensions to surface latent subgroups. K-prototypes handles the dataset's mix of categorical and continuous variables. The cluster count is chosen iteratively from the Silhouette score and the elbow method, which lets the analysis pick out groups that differ in comorbidity profiles, adherence behavior, and how they respond to a regimen.

The cluster assignments then serve two purposes: descriptive interpretation, and engineered features for the supervised models. Cluster labels are one-hot encoded as extra categorical inputs for the tree-based classifiers (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest) and added to the feature space for the temporal networks (RNN, LSTM). A model can then pick up on subgroup-level patterns tied to treatment success or failure, not just individual predictors.

The temporal monitoring dataset got the same treatment before preprocessing: a global missingness audit over every feature, with columns ranked by missing rate. Anything above 80\% missingness was routed to column exclusion. Anything between 50\% and 80\% went to the missing-indicator-with-fill pathway, weighed against its predictive importance. This kind of threshold triage is standard practice; it matches the handling strategy to how severe the missingness is instead of forcing one rule on everything \citep{ref31}.

Patient retention was traced by the share of patients with non-missing adherence data at each interval from M0 through M12. A raw per-month count says little, since retention naturally falls over a six- to twelve-month course, so the decay was expressed as a percentage of the starting cohort at each successive month. This sets the temporal modeling scope. Once fewer than 25\% of patients still have data, that month and everything past it drops out of the feature-level correlation analyses, which keeps the estimates from being driven by a small, non-representative surviving group.

The same Pearson's r check, on available-case observations, was run over the vital signs in the temporal dataset. Strong correlation among vitals is what justifies a multivariate imputation over a univariate one: when vitals move together, Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) can borrow signal from the observed features to fill gaps, and when they don't, MICE buys nothing over mean- or median-fill \citep{ref35}. The vital set was age, heart rate, oxygen saturation, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, weight, height, respiratory rate, and temperature, wherever the temporal records carried them.

To see how the temporal features relate, Pearson correlations were taken on per-patient observed means across Months 0 through 7. For each feature, whether a dose metric (monthly doses taken, cumulative doses taken, percentage adherence), an anthropometric one (weight, height), or a diagnostic result (smear microscopy, Xpert MTB/RIF), the values across a patient's available visits were averaged. Retention characteristics, the last month attended and the total months observed, came from the same window. The point of averaging this way is that it keeps the dropout signal intact. A patient who stops after M4 contributes means from M0 to M4 only, and that short window is itself informative rather than a gap to impute.

Categorical variables in the temporal dataset again used Cram\'{e}r's V, the chi-square-based measure scaled to the [0, 1] interval. We did not mix Pearson and Cram\'{e}r's V into one matrix. Pearson's r assumes interval-level measurement and returns misleading values on nominal or ordinal categories whose spacing is uneven \citep{ref27}, so computing Cram\'{e}r's V on its own for the categorical features sidesteps that and gives interpretable estimates. Columns with fewer than two unique non-null values, or fewer than ten non-null observations, were left out, since association can't be estimated meaningfully there.

A cardinality audit over every categorical feature in the raw dataset showed how much inconsistent coding there was to fix. For each feature, counting the unique raw values quantified the entry inconsistency. When a feature's cardinality ran far past its expected number of valid categories, say a clinical observation field that should hold a handful of standardized codes but instead held hundreds of free-text variants, it was flagged for harmonization in Stage 4. The audit gives the harmonization targets an empirical baseline, measuring the data entry problem before any cleaning touched it.

Whether adherence stays stable over the course was checked with lag-1 Pearson correlations of percentage adherence across consecutive month pairs (M0 to M1 through M7 to M8). That month-to-month strength is what decides if a recurrent architecture, which assumes one time step depends on the last, is even worth it. Weak correlation between consecutive months means a static model on aggregated patient means would tell you just as much; strong correlation is what earns the added complexity of an RNN or LSTM \citep{ref10, ref12}.

\subsection{Missing Data Handling}

Missingness in clinical TB registries is uneven. It comes from inconsistent documentation, facilities with different capacities, and the longitudinal nature of treatment monitoring. When the mechanism behind the gaps varies from feature to feature, forcing one imputation strategy on all of them is statistically wrong. This study instead uses a diagnostic-first pipeline that classifies each variable's missingness mechanism and then routes it to one of four strategies: column exclusion, listwise deletion, missing indicator with fill, or stochastic Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) (Figure~\ref{fig:missing_data_pipeline}) \citep{ref31, ref32}.

The first diagnostic step runs Little's MCAR test at the dataset level to ask whether data are Missing Completely at Random (MCAR). If they were, missingness would be independent of every observed and unobserved value, the complete cases would form an unbiased subsample, and listwise deletion would be fine. The test came back significant ($p < 0.05$), which rejects MCAR: the missingness is systematically tied to the observed data. That rules out deleting cases wholesale \citep{ref32}.

Little's test gives one omnibus verdict for the whole dataset, so classifying each column's mechanism needs its own step. Each variable's missingness indicator is then checked against the observed data to assign a column-level mechanism. For temporal (monthly) variables, Spearman rank correlations relate the column's monthly missing rate to treatment month; a correlation that rises monotonically marks a dropout pattern, which is Missing Not at Random (MNAR). For static baseline variables, point-biserial correlations relate the binary is-missing indicator to the observed features. A significant association says other observed variables explain the missingness, so it is Missing at Random (MAR); a non-significant one points to MNAR. Classifying column by column instead of accepting one dataset-level verdict is what lets the pipeline fit each handling strategy to the statistics of each variable \citep{ref31, ref32}.

The column exclusion and MICE eligibility decisions lean on a Random Forest classifier trained on available cases, meaning rows where the treatment outcome is observed. For ranking only, features are filled with column medians, a coarse fill but enough to estimate importance. Variables in the bottom quartile of importance become candidates for soft exclusion, while those above the median get priority for stochastic imputation \citep{ref35}.

A hard threshold drops any variable over 80\% missingness outright, importance aside, since nothing reliable can be imputed at that sparsity. A soft threshold also drops variables past 50\% missingness whose importance sits in the bottom quartile, because imputing a low-value sparse variable adds more synthetic variance than information. Temporal variables escape both thresholds: their structural missingness is the longitudinal design of TB monitoring at work, not a data quality fault \citep{ref37}.

Listwise deletion stays available for a narrow case: a variable confirmed MCAR at the column level, under 15\% missing, and leaving more than 500 patients after deletion. There the complete cases are an unbiased subsample, so deletion gives unbiased estimates and avoids the variance distortion imputation adds. No variable in this dataset met all three at once, so the pathway never fired \citep{ref32}.

Variables classified MNAR, plus every temporal feature whatever its mechanism, get a two-part treatment. A binary indicator (\texttt{is\_missing\_[variable]}) is added to encode the absence itself. The original variable is then filled: last observed value carried forward for temporal variables, column median for continuous static ones, column mode for categorical static ones. The absence is treated as a signal, not noise to paper over. A missing Month 9 weight in TB monitoring often means the patient dropped out, and the indicator hands that clinical pattern to the model. Backward-fill is barred from temporal imputation, since letting a future measurement bleed into an earlier time step would leak information the model can't legitimately have at inference \citep{ref34, ref36, ref33}.

Four vital signs cleared both bars, confirmed MAR and ranked above median importance: oxygen saturation, heart rate, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. These are imputed with stochastic MICE using an ExtraTreesRegressor as the conditional model. ExtraTreesRegressor is a nonparametric tree-based estimator that picks up non-linear relationships in mixed-type clinical data without needing standardized variables, much like the missForest framework \citep{ref35}, and it tends to beat linear and $k$-nearest-neighbor imputers on such data. To track the fraction of missing information per variable, the MICE iteration count scales as $\max(20, \min(100, \lfloor \text{miss\_rate} \times 100 \rfloor))$, giving high-sparsity features room to converge while capping compute. The idea mirrors Von Hippel's rule that the number of imputations should roughly match the percentage of missing information \citep{ref38}, applied here to iterations rather than to the count of imputed datasets. Stochastic rather than deterministic imputation keeps the imputed variables' natural spread instead of collapsing them onto conditional means \citep{ref1}.

\subsection{Data Quality Assurance}

A layered quality assurance protocol keeps the supervised pipeline reliable, spanning schema validation, clinical bounds checking, temporal consistency, and post-processing validation.

\subsubsection*{Schema and Column Validation}

Column names are normalized to \texttt{snake\_case}: whitespace stripped, text lowercased, special characters mapped to word equivalents, so \texttt{M0 \%Adherence} becomes \texttt{m0\_pct\_adherence}. Duplicate and entirely null columns are flagged and deduplicated before modeling. Four patient- and facility-identifying columns stay in the export file for traceability but are kept out of all model inputs for privacy.

\subsubsection*{Date Integrity Correction}

A dedicated correction pass handles the recording errors common in clinical registries. A future date of birth (year past the data year) is pulled back 100 years. A date of birth before 1920 is moved forward 100 years when that lands the age in a plausible 0 to 110 range. Transposed digits, such as 2990 for 1990, are caught against adjacent records. Date pairs are forced into order: treatment start cannot precede diagnosis, and an outcome date cannot precede treatment start. Any missing date takes the cohort median date for that variable.

\subsubsection*{Temporal Consistency}

Every fill in the longitudinal structure is forward-only, which is what keeps the temporal data from leaking. A Month 4 observation can carry forward to Month 5, but a Month 6 measurement can never inform an earlier step the model is supposed to predict without it. After MICE, a monotonicity repair pass undoes any drop in \texttt{cumulative\_doses\_taken} the multivariate imputer introduced, so each patient's cumulative dose never decreases across treatment months.

\subsubsection*{Categorical Harmonization Audit}

A cardinality audit of the raw categorical features confirmed how widespread the entry inconsistency was. The \texttt{chest\_x\_ray\_at\_case\_notification} field held over 300 unique free-text entries and \texttt{co\_morbidities} more than 150, while even nominally binary fields like \texttt{sex} and \texttt{outcome} showed 5 to 12 raw representations apiece, the residue of treatment centers abbreviating things their own way. That is what prompted a 17-field harmonization pass mapping free-text and inconsistently coded entries to standardized categories before analysis.

\subsubsection*{Missing Data Diagnostics and Feature Importance}

The diagnostic-first missing data pipeline (detailed in the Missing Data Handling subsection) adds one more validation layer. Little's MCAR test runs at the dataset level. Per-column mechanisms come from Spearman rank correlations to catch temporal dropout and point-biserial correlations to tell MAR from MNAR. A Random Forest trained on available cases ranks predictive importance, which decides where each variable gets routed.

\subsubsection*{Post-Processing Verification}

Before the cleaned dataset goes to any model, an automated routine checks four things: (1) identifier columns are still present for traceability, (2) no scaled or normalized values have crept in, so everything stays in original clinical units, (3) categorical labels remain human-readable strings rather than integer codes, and (4) no one-hot columns have appeared. Passing all four confirms that encoding, scaling, and tensor construction were left entirely to the model-specific scripts that run after the split.

\subsubsection*{Leakage Prevention and Reproducibility}

Every validation step exists to keep test-set information out of training. The patient-level stratified split comes before any transformation. Scalers, imputers, and encoders are fit on the training set alone, then pushed to the test and validation sets through \texttt{reindex()} so the column sets match exactly. Temporal fills are forward-only, with backward-fill explicitly disabled. For reproducibility, the split is fully deterministic under a seeded random state, and the fitted scaler parameters are checked by SHA-256 hash to confirm independent runs preprocess identically. An audit trail (\texttt{missing\_data\_report.json}) logs the decision pathway, mechanism classification, and rationale for every column.

\subsection{Identification of Variables Associated With Treatment Outcomes}

For the objective of finding which variables track with treatment outcomes, the study pairs descriptive association analysis with model-derived explanation. The descriptive side summarizes which demographic, clinical, diagnostic, and temporal variables separate the success and failure groups. The supervised models then estimate how those variables work together to predict the outcome. Both are needed because risk in TB treatment is multidimensional: a variable can look weak on its own yet matter once it sits alongside adherence, diagnostic, geographic, or monitoring information.

Bridging predictive performance and clinical interpretability falls to Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), a post-hoc method that measures each variable's marginal contribution to the model's output \citep{lundberg2017unified}. SHAP shows which features pushed a patient-level prediction toward ``Success'' (Cured, Completed) or ``Failure'' (Failed, Died, Lost to Follow-up; \citealp{ref15}). For clinical review, that means seeing what the model leaned on, whether drug resistance, comorbidities, adherence gaps, diagnostic results, or a delayed diagnosis. These explanations describe how the model behaved; they do not prove clinical causality, and that line between computational attribution and medical judgment is kept deliberately clear \citep{ref26, ref27}.

\subsection{Temporal Modeling}

TB treatment runs 6 to 12 months, so this study adds temporal modeling for the longitudinal monitoring data. A static model trained on baseline features alone can miss how adherence, follow-up attendance, and lab measurements shift over the course. The temporal models are evaluated to give month-by-month risk estimates from treatment Month 0 (M0) through Month 12 (M12).

These models run only on the 599-patient longitudinal dataset from the Data Acquisition and Preprocessing subsection (see also Table~\ref{tab:dataset_comparison}), the one the Pharmacy Co-authors compiled by hand from facility monitoring forms. It is a separate dataset from the 7,389-patient DOH-CAR registry the static classifiers use. After the eight-stage temporal pipeline, each patient has up to 8 usable monthly timesteps, M0 to M7. Anything past M7 is left out of the model inputs: fewer than 25\% of patients had observations there (Table~\ref{tab:retention_stats_by_month}), and a surviving subset that small and skewed gives unreliable estimates. As before, encoding, scaling, and 3-dimensional tensor preparation wait for the post-split, model-specific scripts so nothing leaks.

Temporal modeling went through two iterations, v1 and v2. Both share the same dataset lineage; v2 is the corrected, modeling-ready preparation that a methodological review of v1 produced. The v1 pipeline served as a modeling baseline, with patient-level stratified splitting and progressive temporal expansion across M0 to M12. Review then flagged several things that could weaken its clinical defensibility: late-treatment fields raising the risk of outcome leakage, inconsistent handling of imputed or unreliable outcome labels, and cleaning and temporal feature construction that had not been audited as carefully.

Version 2 (v2) fixed those issues and retrained. It builds on the audited output of the eight-stage cleaning pipeline from Data Acquisition and Preprocessing, limits supervised learning to patients whose outcome is actually observed, and strips out leakage-prone and administrative fields such as outcome-date fields and late timeline variables that wouldn't exist at early inference. Temporal predictors are encoded two ways: as sequence inputs for the deep models and as engineered summaries for the tree-based ones. The deep architecture is a Hybrid Bidirectional LSTM (Bi-LSTM), chosen to model long-term dependencies without the vanishing gradients of a plain RNN \citep{ref11}; its recurrent structure fits the strong month-to-month adherence dependence the lag-1 analysis confirmed \citep{ref12}. The tree-based baselines instead flatten the monthly values and bolt on aggregates, trends, latest-observed values, and missingness indicators. Class imbalance is handled by training-only resampling such as SMOTE-ENN. The hybrid framing stays so temporal predictions can be set beside strong static learners like XGBoost, which holds up better across the different patient subgroups \citep{ref10}.

\subsection{Supervised Learning Model Development and Validation}

At the core, supervised models are trained and validated to classify treatment outcomes. The study uses ensemble tree-based algorithms that handle complex non-linear interactions among mixed-type clinical variables and deal with class imbalance natively: XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost,ref16}, LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm}, and Random Forest \citep{breiman2001random}. These three came out of a preliminary run over six algorithms that also tried Logistic Regression, SVM, and Gradient Boosting. The three that were dropped either discriminated poorly (CV-AUC $\leq 0.63$) or had near-zero recall on the minority failure class, which makes them unsafe to deploy in a CDSS. Each classifier is run under two sampling strategies: no resampling with native class-weighting, and SMOTE with Edited Nearest Neighbors (SMOTE-ENN), which grows the training failure class by 433\% to a near-balanced 0.70:1 ratio.

\subsubsection{Feature Set Versions}

Three feature sets are evaluated in sequence to see how much each extra clinical and temporal group of features actually adds. The \textit{baseline} set (11 features) is the core demographic and diagnostic block: age, sex, anatomical site, bacteriologic status, microscopy result, registration group, source of patient, TB type, province, days to treatment, and year of registration. The \textit{improved} set (14 features) adds three facility-level geographic variables to the baseline: city/municipality, treatment health facility, and screening/diagnosing health facility. The \textit{extended} set (21 features) further adds seven temporal and diagnostic delay features derived from date engineering: diagnosis month, diagnosis quarter, diagnosis season (using the Philippine three-season meteorological calendar: \textit{Tag-lamig} for cool dry December to February, \textit{Tag-init} for hot dry March to May, and \textit{Tag-ulan} for the rainy season June to November), days from screening to diagnosis, days from diagnosis to microscopy result, days from diagnosis to rapid diagnostic test (RDT) result, and RDT result (binned from 42 raw variants into three classes: Detected, Not Detected, and Not Done). The date of outcome was excluded from all feature sets to prevent causal leakage, since this field is generated after the treatment result.

\subsubsection{Data Partitioning and Class Imbalance}

A stratified 70/20/10 patient-level split divides the data into training (70\%), test (20\%), and holdout validation (10\%), holding the class proportions across all three \citep{hastie2009elements}. The heavy imbalance, roughly 86\% success against 14\% failure, is corrected on the training set only, using one of three oversamplers. SMOTE \citep{chawla2002smote} interpolates synthetic minority-class points. SMOTE-ENN \citep{batista2004study,nishat2022smoteenn} runs SMOTE and then Edited Nearest Neighbors undersampling to clear out ambiguous borderline cases. SMOTE-Tomek \citep{batista2004study} runs SMOTE and then removes Tomek links to clean the boundary. Resampling never touches the test or validation sets, which keep their original distributions so the evaluation stays unbiased \citep{salmi2024imbalanced}.

\subsubsection{Cross-Validation and Model Selection}

Each configuration is scored by five-fold stratified cross-validation on the training data, which estimates how well it generalizes and yields a mean and standard deviation of ROC-AUC across folds \citep{hastie2009elements}. Since the clinical goal is catching at-risk patients early, selection uses a composite metric, $\text{score} = 0.6 \times \text{AUC} + 0.4 \times \text{Recall(Failure)}$, that favors discrimination while rewarding sensitivity to the minority failure class. Whichever configuration scores highest across the feature versions and resamplers is then checked on the held-out 10\% validation set for an unbiased real-world estimate.

Alongside the composite metric, each configuration also reports the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) as a supplementary check. Where accuracy or a class-specific F1 can mislead, MCC reads all four quadrants of the confusion matrix at once and stays informative under heavy imbalance. It lands in $[-1, +1]$: $+1$ for perfect prediction, $0$ for random, $-1$ for complete inversion \citep{chicco2020advantages}. Seeing MCC next to the composite score makes the trade-off from aggressive resampling visible, since something like SMOTE-ENN can lift failure recall while adding false positives that drive up overall error.

\subsubsection{Performance Evaluation}

Performance is read across several metrics, each catching a different facet of classification quality under imbalance. Treatment failure is only 13.8\% of the cohort, so leaning on any one metric, accuracy above all, can mislead: a classifier that simply says ``Success'' every time hits 86.2\% accuracy and catches not a single failure. The framework therefore computes discriminative, threshold-dependent, and calibration-aware metrics together.

Every metric comes from the confusion matrix, with treatment failure as the positive class. On the held-out test set, each model gives four quadrants: true positives (TP, failures correctly predicted), false negatives (FN, failures missed), false positives (FP, successes wrongly flagged as failures), and true negatives (TN, successes correctly identified). The metrics below follow from these:

\textbf{Accuracy} ($\frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}$) measures the overall proportion of correct predictions. It is intuitive but is reported with the caveat that it is dominated by the majority class under 86:14 imbalance.

\textbf{Precision} ($\frac{TP}{TP + FP}$), also called positive predictive value, quantifies the proportion of failure alerts that correspond to actual failures. Low precision means a high rate of false alarms, which erodes clinician trust in the CDSS.

\textbf{Recall} ($\frac{TP}{TP + FN}$), also called sensitivity or true positive rate, measures the proportion of actual failures the model detects. In the TB-DOTS context, a missed failure carries higher clinical cost than a false alert, because undetected treatment breakdown can lead to disease progression, drug resistance, and continued community transmission.

\textbf{Specificity} ($\frac{TN}{TN + FP}$), the true negative rate, measures the proportion of actual successes correctly identified. High specificity keeps patients on track from being unnecessarily flagged for review.

\textbf{F1 Score} ($2 \times \frac{Precision \times Recall}{Precision + Recall}$) is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. It provides a single metric that penalizes extreme imbalance between the two. F1 is useful when comparing models under different resampling strategies.

\textbf{Balanced Accuracy} ($\frac{Recall + Specificity}{2}$) averages the sensitivity and specificity, giving a metric that is robust to class imbalance by construction. A balanced accuracy of 0.50 is equivalent to random guessing regardless of the class distribution.

\textbf{Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC)} evaluates the model's discriminative ability across all possible decision thresholds. The ROC curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate at each threshold, and the AUC summarizes the curve as a single scalar. An ROC-AUC of 1.0 indicates perfect separation; 0.5 indicates random chance. ROC-AUC is threshold-independent and serves as the primary ranking metric for model comparison, but it can be optimistic under severe class imbalance because the false positive rate is dominated by the large majority class.

\textbf{Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (PR-AUC)} addresses this limitation by plotting precision against recall across thresholds, ignoring true negatives entirely. PR-AUC is more informative than ROC-AUC when the positive class is rare \citep{davis2006relationship}. The no-skill baseline for PR-AUC equals the prevalence of the positive class (0.138), giving a natural reference point.

\textbf{Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)} is computed as:
\[
MCC = \frac{TP \times TN - FP \times FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}
\]
MCC accounts for all four confusion-matrix quadrants at once and remains informative under class imbalance. It gives a coefficient in $[-1, +1]$ where $+1$ is perfect prediction, $0$ is random, and $-1$ is complete inverse prediction \citep{chicco2020advantages}. Unlike accuracy or F1, MCC is symmetric with respect to class swapping and does not inflate under majority-class dominance.

\textbf{Threshold optimization.} A 0.50 cutoff need not match clinical priorities under heavy imbalance, so each model gets a threshold search. Candidate thresholds from 0.10 to 0.90 are scored by composite objectives that encode different priorities: \textit{balanced} (40\% F1, 30\% recall, 30\% specificity), \textit{failure\_recall} (55\% failure recall, 25\% balanced accuracy, 20\% F1), and \textit{specificity} (50\% specificity, 30\% F1, 20\% recall). A model's reported metrics use whichever threshold maximizes the relevant objective.

\textbf{Model selection} uses a composite score weighting discriminative performance and clinical utility:
\[
S = 0.6 \times \text{ROC-AUC} + 0.4 \times \text{Recall(Failure)}
\]
This puts most of the weight on ranking ability (AUC) but still rewards sensitivity to the minority failure class. The top configuration by this score becomes the recommended model.

\textbf{Validation strategy.} Performance is estimated at three levels to guard against overfitting and gauge generalization. Five-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set gives the mean and standard deviation of ROC-AUC across folds, a stability check. The 20\% held-out test set carries the primary evaluation behind all the metrics above, on data the model never saw in training or preprocessing. A final 10\% holdout set is reserved for the recommended model alone, to confirm the test-set numbers aren't just an artifact of one split. Scaling, encoding, and imputation are all fit on the training set and then applied to the test and validation sets, so nothing from the evaluation data feeds back into training.

For reproducibility, every experiment runs through one centralized pipeline that standardizes training, cross-validation, and evaluation. It exports the metrics and visualizations straight to native \LaTeX{} for this manuscript.

\subsection{Development of a Clinical Decision Support Tool}

The validated models are embedded in a Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) built for healthcare providers, with responsibilities kept distinct. The predictive model estimates the probability of treatment success or failure, the SHAP layer names the factors behind that prediction, the interface shows the risk and those factors, and the clinician reads it all in the context of the patient. In practice the provider sees a dashboard with the failure and success probabilities, a risk-factor view that surfaces what drove the score (adherence gaps, lab results, diagnostic delays), and live updates: feed in new follow-up data like a monthly weight or smear result and the prediction refreshes, so review can continue across the whole treatment. The CDSS is meant for risk stratification and explanation, not as an autonomous system that diagnoses or prescribes.

\subsection{Small Language Model (SLM)-Based Narrative Explanation Layer}

To make the explanations easier for clinical users to read, the CDSS adds a small language model (SLM) as a constrained narrative layer that turns model outputs into plain clinical text. The SHAP feature contributions are still the explanation of record. The SLM is only a presentation layer; it is not a predictor, a diagnostic engine, or a source of new clinical inference \citep{ref39, ref41}. It runs after prediction and changes nothing upstream: not the training, not the inference probabilities, not the risk thresholds, not the SHAP values. In the implemented pipeline, the web client assembles a structured interpretation request from selected patient context fields, the predicted treatment-failure probability, and the ranked contribution signals, then posts it to a dedicated endpoint that streams the narrative back into the interface.

The SLM is confined to explanation. It does not infer new clinical causes, write prescriptions, suggest regimen changes, or stand in for clinician judgment. All it does is make the SHAP-grounded attribution easier to read, so a provider can see why the CDSS flagged a patient and decide whether a closer look is warranted. Keeping the roles this separate lines up with how medical-domain SLMs are currently framed and with health AI governance expectations for bounded decision support, and it keeps interpretability anchored to the original attribution signal \citep{ref40, ref42, ref44}. The final call still belongs to the treating professional.

\subsection{Missing Data Handling Outcomes}

Of the 44 features identified with missing data, 15 were excluded via column exclusion. All 15 exceeded the hard missingness threshold of 80\%, including near-complete variables such as tuberculosis culture (99.8\%), other laboratory tests (99.8\%), and tuberculin skin test (93.5\%). One more variable, the raw blood pressure string field, was soft-excluded at 67.3\% missingness and below-median predictive importance because it had already been parsed into the structured systolic and diastolic blood pressure variables. All 599 patient records were kept in the dataset, with zero row deletion. Full sample retention matters given the small cohort size and the severe class imbalance of 86.2\% treatment success versus 13.8\% failure. Listwise deletion under these conditions would have made the minority class representation problem worse \citep{ref32}.

The listwise deletion pathway was not activated. The dataset-level MCAR test was rejected, and no individual variable met all three conditions of confirmed MCAR mechanism, fewer than 15\% missing observations, and a post-deletion sample size above 500. Twenty-five variables were routed to the missing indicator with fill strategy: eight temporal monthly features (including weight, percentage adherence, cumulative doses taken, and monthly smear results) and 17 static baseline variables with confirmed MNAR patterns. Four variables (oxygen saturation, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure) were imputed via stochastic MICE after confirmation of a MAR mechanism. Their missingness indicators were significantly associated with visit attendance under the point-biserial correlation procedure described above. This is a clinically plausible MAR mechanism, since vital sign recording depends on whether the patient attended the monthly monitoring visit rather than on the unobserved values of the measurements themselves. The concentration of stochastic imputation on this small set of high-value features reflects the diagnostic-first design of the pipeline, which reserves computationally intensive probabilistic imputation for variables where the assumptions required for its validity are met \citep{ref31, ref33}.

\subsection{Longitudinal Trends (2015--2025)}

An analysis of annual treatment outcomes from 2015 to 2025 revealed significant temporal shifts, reflecting both programmatic improvements and external systemic shocks:

\begin{enumerate}

\subsection{Diagnostic and Treatment Intervals}

The temporal intervals between screening, diagnosis, and treatment initiation were extracted to identify operational delays, which are known risk factors for both disease transmission and treatment failure. Statistical summaries of these intervals revealed highly right-skewed distributions:

XGBoost with SMOTE-ENN was selected as the recommended model ($S = 0.699$). Although its overall accuracy was lower (55.5\%) due to SMOTE-ENN's aggressive resampling of the training set, and its test-set AUC was moderate (0.680), this trade-off is clinically justified: in a TB CDSS, a missed failure (false negative) results in undetected treatment breakdown and potential disease progression or drug resistance, whereas a false alert triggers additional clinical review --- a lower-cost error. Among models without resampling, XGBoost (no sampling) achieved the highest test-set AUC (0.712) and CV-AUC ($0.702 \pm 0.009$) across all six configurations, with a competitive failure recall of 56.6\%. LightGBM (no sampling) performed comparably ($\text{AUC} = 0.708$, recall$= 55.2\%$), providing a useful corroborating model. The three configurations not selected --- Random Forest (both sampling strategies) and LightGBM with SMOTE-ENN --- showed lower composite scores, driven by weaker failure recall relative to their AUC costs.

The MCC values reported in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} reveal the cost of this recall-focused selection. XGBoost with SMOTE-ENN achieved an MCC of 0.175, the lowest among all six test-set configurations. In contrast, XGBoost without resampling yielded the highest MCC (0.258), reflecting its superior balance across all four confusion matrix quadrants. This divergence illustrates a fundamental tension in imbalanced clinical classification: SMOTE-ENN raises failure recall by generating a large volume of synthetic minority-class samples during training, but the resulting model produces proportionally more false positives on the real test distribution, which MCC penalizes symmetrically. Accordingly, the recommended XGBoost with SMOTE-ENN configuration is best suited for a screening-oriented deployment where catching every at-risk patient takes precedence over minimizing false alerts, whereas XGBoost without resampling is the preferred choice when the operational cost of unnecessary clinical reviews is a binding constraint.

The month-to-month stability of adherence behavior in the temporal dataset provides empirical support for the choice of temporal model architecture. \section{Discussion}

This study investigated the use of machine learning models to predict treatment outcomes---Success or Failure---of PTB patients under the DOH-CAR TB-DOTS program, using monthly patient monitoring data alongside baseline clinical features. Temporal modeling was implemented in two iterations. The v1 iteration served as an initial baseline, but its results demonstrate a common pitfall in imbalanced clinical prediction: high accuracy can be achieved by favoring the majority Success class while failing to detect true treatment failures. This is reflected by v1 specificity of 0.0000 on the held-out test split, indicating that Failure identification was not reliable under the initial workflow.

The corrected v2 iteration improved the methodological defensibility of temporal prediction by addressing leakage risk, outcome-label handling, and temporal feature preparation. On the v2 held-out test split, the SMOTE-ENN tree models achieved consistent performance and identified 5 of 6 Failure cases while correctly classifying 36 of 38 Success cases. Random Forest exhibited the strongest held-out ranking performance among the tree models (ROC-AUC 0.9693, PR-AUC 0.9950), while LightGBM reported the strongest cross-validated balanced accuracy in the saved artifacts. The v2 Hybrid Bi-LSTM baseline detected all Failure cases on the held-out test split (specificity 1.0000) but produced more false Failure alerts among true Success cases, lowering overall accuracy. This tradeoff can be acceptable in a screening-oriented CDSS where missing high-risk patients is more costly than additional review workload, but it must be managed to avoid over-alerting.

The study contributes in several ways. By integrating temporal monitoring data with static baseline features, it demonstrates that patient-level longitudinal information improves predictive performance over static data alone. The application of SMOTE-ENN and advanced data augmentation techniques for Bi-LSTM addresses the extreme class imbalance inherent in TB treatment outcomes, enhancing the model's capacity to detect high-risk patients. Furthermore, the study shows why prediction must be paired with explanation in a clinical decision support context: risk estimates become easier to inspect and review when healthcare providers can see the patient-level factors that contributed to the alert. SHAP-based explanations and bounded SLM summaries therefore serve as the interpretability layer that connects model output to clinician review without transferring decision authority away from healthcare professionals.

These findings suggest that the v2 temporal modeling outputs may serve as a basis for further evaluation within TB monitoring workflows, particularly for earlier identification of patients who may require closer review. For deployment-oriented performance, the v2 tree models (particularly Random Forest and LightGBM with SMOTE-ENN) provide a strong balance of Failure detection and overall correctness, while the Hybrid Bi-LSTM baseline may be useful when a more conservative screening stance is desired. However, any use in clinical or programmatic settings should first undergo external validation, prospective testing, and review by healthcare professionals to assess safety, calibration, and workflow fit. Future research should focus on larger, multi-region datasets to enhance generalization, evaluate temporal calibration over time, and explore hybrid modeling approaches that combine deep temporal representations with tree-based learners. Additionally, enhancing interpretability of temporal dynamics (e.g., attention over treatment months) may improve clinician trust and adoption.

The integration of these CDSS artifacts represents a shift from retrospective reporting to proactive clinical management within the DOH-CAR TB-DOTS program. While traditional NTP protocols typically identify treatment failure only after it has occurred, the predictive outputs allow for pre-emptive intervention during the critical early months of therapy. The use of the \textit{Full Breakdown} report specifically addresses the "black-box" nature of ensemble models, empowering healthcare workers to validate the AI's logic against their clinical experience. Furthermore, the \textit{First Step} document ensures that high predictive accuracy translates directly into improved patient care pathways, potentially reducing the incidence of MDR-TB emergence caused by unrecognized non-adherence. The integration of SHAP and Med-Gemma directly addresses the skepticism often associated with AI in healthcare. By providing both a statistical breakdown and a medical narrative, the CDSS facilitates a 'human-in-the-loop' approach. This ensures that the DOH-CAR health workers can cross-reference the model’s findings with their own expertise, thereby increasing the likelihood of successful clinical adoption and improved patient outcomes.

\subsection{Recommendations}

Healthcare facilities and policymakers should consider integrating tree-based models---particularly Random Forest and LightGBM with SMOTE-ENN resampling---into existing DOH-CAR TB-DOTS monitoring programs as a decision-support layer for early risk stratification. These models demonstrated balanced detection of treatment failure on the held-out test set while maintaining high overall accuracy, offering a practical basis for identifying patients who may require closer clinical review. Deployment should be preceded by external validation on independent multi-facility datasets, prospective testing under real-world workflow conditions, and review by healthcare professionals to assess calibration, safety, and fit with existing clinical practice.

The explainability layer warrants formal evaluation before any clinical deployment. A preliminary internal evaluation on the 44-patient test cohort found that the narrative layer consistently failed to echo the model's SHAP attribution when operating without access to the attribution signal: no cases passed the faithfulness criterion under the blind condition (0 of 44), regardless of the SLM backend used. Faithfulness improved substantially when SHAP contribution signals were included in the prompt, confirming that the narrative layer functions as a constrained verbalizer of the model's reasoning rather than an independent reasoner. Based on these findings, it is recommended that future work extend the faithfulness evaluation to a larger, multi-facility patient cohort, incorporate multiple SLM backends, and include clinician-rated readability, trust, and actionability as paired outcome measures to establish the evidence base needed for safe deployment of the explanation component.

\subsection{Safety Scope and Interpretation Limits}

The explanation layer is intended to support readability and interpretation of model reasoning by translating contribution signals into clinician-readable text, but its outputs must be interpreted within clear safety boundaries. SHAP explanations describe how features increased or decreased the model-predicted risk; they do not establish causal mechanisms of disease progression or prove that changing a highlighted factor will alter the patient's outcome. This distinction is important in high-stakes settings where generative outputs may otherwise be over-interpreted beyond their evidentiary basis \citep{ref42, ref43}.

Accordingly, the system is positioned as decision support: it flags risk, surfaces contributing factors, and summarizes model behavior while preserving clinician authority for diagnosis and treatment decisions. This framing is consistent with contemporary reporting and governance guidance for AI-assisted clinical prediction systems, where transparency, bounded use, and human oversight remain central requirements \citep{ref44}.

The preliminary faithfulness evaluation described above supports this bounded framing by showing that the narrative layer depends on SHAP contribution signals rather than reconstructing model attribution from patient context alone. Accordingly, the operational design retains SHAP as the primary explanation surface and treats the narrative output as a readability aid rather than a substitute for the attribution itself.

\section{Conclusion}

Tuberculosis treatment in the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR) is shaped by very different patient situations, gaps in record-keeping, and disruptions to the health program. These are problems that simple one-variable analyses cannot fully explain. To turn this raw data into something useful for prediction, the study first built a clear and repeatable cleaning pipeline for two related datasets: a 7{,}389-patient DOH-CAR registry and a 599-patient month-by-month monitoring set. The pipeline standardized formats, removed patient identifiers, and handled missing values by first checking why each variable was missing (MCAR, MAR, or MNAR) before applying one of four matching strategies. Because the cleaned data was trustworthy rather than blindly filled in, the descriptive and exploratory analyses could clearly show outcome trends over the years, the rise in deaths and lost follow-ups during the COVID-19 period, and the patient-level, time-based, and operational patterns that later guided model design.

These cleaned signals fed directly into how well the models performed. On the static dataset, XGBoost with SMOTE-ENN gave the strongest screening-focused setup (composite score $S = 0.699$; failure recall 72.9\%), while XGBoost without resampling produced the best-calibrated risk ranking (test AUC 0.712; MCC 0.258), offering two different setups depending on how the tool will be used. The longitudinal models pushed performance further: after the v2 pipeline fixed the data leakage and label-handling issues that had limited v1, the SMOTE-ENN tree models (Random Forest, LightGBM, and XGBoost) reached 0.9318 accuracy and 0.8333 specificity, correctly catching five of six failure cases in the held-out test set, while the Hybrid Bi-LSTM caught every failure case but produced more false alarms. Building on these predictions, SHAP-based feature explanations and a bounded SLM narrative layer were combined into a Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS) that shows each patient's risk level, the factors driving that risk, and updated estimates as new monthly data come in.

These results are strong enough to be clinically promising, but not yet ready for actual clinical use. Since the test cases came from only one regional registry, and since the narrative layer's faithfulness was only checked on a small group of 44 patients, the system must first go through external validation using data from other facilities, prospective testing in real clinical workflows, and review by healthcare professionals to confirm safety, calibration, and fit. The narrative explanation layer will also need a larger faithfulness study before it can be safely used in clinical practice. Once these conditions are met, the framework and the published pseudonymized version of the DOH-CAR PTB Treatment Outcome Dataset may only then be able to support both regional TB control efforts and future research on explainable clinical prediction.
